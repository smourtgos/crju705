The Recipe

Every question tonight, the same four steps:

  1. Question. Name the two groups and the outcome
  2. Count or describe. count() for yes/no; group averages for a number
  3. Test. Frequentist always; Bayesian too when the outcome is yes/no
  4. Say it. The honest sentence · how big, in real units · one thing this test cannot tell you

Which Test?

The outcome is… Example Test
yes/no arrested again? searched? released? prop.test() and bayes_prop_test()
a number age, income, number of prior arrests t.test(outcome ~ group, data = )

That table covers every question in tonight’s lecture and lab, and a surprising share of what gets published in criminal justice journals.

Example 1 · Reentry: Does Money at Release Keep People Out of Jail?

The Study

  • 432 men released from Maryland state prisons in the 1970s, followed for one year
  • Half were randomly assigned to receive financial aid after release; half were not
  • Outcome: arrested again within the year (1 = yes)
  • One row = one released man

Question: did the aid group get re-arrested less often? Two groups, yes/no outcome: prop.test() and bayes_prop_test().

Count

rossi |> count(fin, arrest)
# A tibble: 4 × 3
  fin   arrest     n
  <chr>  <dbl> <int>
1 no         0   150
2 no         1    66
3 yes        0   168
4 yes        1    48
  • Aid (fin = yes): 48 re-arrested out of 48 + 168 = 216 (22.2%)
  • No aid: 66 out of 66 + 150 = 216 (30.6%)

Aid first all the way through, so every difference reads Aid minus No aid.

Test: prop.test()

prop.test(x = c(48, 66), n = c(216, 216))

    2-sample test for equality of proportions with continuity correction

data:  c(48, 66) out of c(216, 216)
X-squared = 3.4439, df = 1, p-value = 0.06349
alternative hypothesis: two.sided
95 percent confidence interval:
 -0.170713151  0.004046484
sample estimates:
   prop 1    prop 2 
0.2222222 0.3055556

Reading It

  • Interval: about 17 points lower to 0.4 points higher. Zero is (just) inside
  • p = 0.063. Above 0.05: fail to reject the null

A funder reading only the p-value writes “no significant effect” and cuts the program. Sound familiar? It is Session 6’s recidivism trial, except this one is real. Now ask the other question.

Test: bayes_prop_test()

bayes_prop_test(x = c(48, 66), n = c(216, 216))

    Bayesian test of two proportions 

data, group 1:  48 of 216 (22.2%)
data, group 2:  66 of 216 (30.6%)
prior:          know-nothing (1 yes, 1 no), same for both groups

                         estimate      95% credible interval
group 1                  22.5%         17.2% to 28.2%
group 2                  30.7%         24.8% to 37.0%
difference (1 minus 2)   -8.2 points   -16.5 to 0.0 points

Probability group 1 is higher than group 2: 0.03 
Probability group 1 is lower than group 2:  0.97

Where Is Zero?

Say It

  • The sentence: given these data, there is about a 97% probability that men who got aid were re-arrested at a lower rate
  • How big: about 22 of every 100 men with aid were re-arrested, against about 31 of every 100 without: roughly 8 fewer re-arrests per 100 people released
  • What this test cannot tell you: whether the effect lasts past one year, or whether aid would work the same way today

“Not significant” is not “no effect.” Here the best estimate is an 8-point drop, and the data say it is very probably real.

Example 2 · Pretrial Release: Does a Job Get You Released?

The Data

  • 5,226 arrests by Toronto police for possessing small amounts of marijuana, 1997 to 2002
  • Outcome: released with a summons (instead of held for a bail hearing)
  • Group: was the person employed?
  • One row = one arrest

Count

arrests |> count(employed, released)
# A tibble: 4 × 3
  employed released     n
  <chr>    <chr>    <int>
1 No       No         349
2 No       Yes        766
3 Yes      No         543
4 Yes      Yes       3568
  • Employed: 3,568 released out of 3,568 + 543 = 4,111 (86.8%)
  • Not employed: 766 out of 766 + 349 = 1,115 (68.7%)

Test: prop.test()

prop.test(x = c(3568, 766), n = c(4111, 1115))

Test: prop.test()


    2-sample test for equality of proportions with continuity correction

data:  c(3568, 766) out of c(4111, 1115)
X-squared = 201.54, df = 1, p-value < 2.2e-16
alternative hypothesis: two.sided
95 percent confidence interval:
 0.1512300 0.2106097
sample estimates:
   prop 1    prop 2 
0.8679153 0.6869955

Test: bayes_prop_test()

bayes_prop_test(x = c(3568, 766), n = c(4111, 1115))

Test: bayes_prop_test()


    Bayesian test of two proportions 

data, group 1:  3,568 of 4,111 (86.8%)
data, group 2:  766 of 1,115 (68.7%)
prior:          know-nothing (1 yes, 1 no), same for both groups

                         estimate      95% credible interval
group 1                  86.8%         85.7% to 87.8%
group 2                  68.7%         65.9% to 71.4%
difference (1 minus 2)   18.1 points   15.2 to 21.0 points

Probability group 1 is higher than group 2: more than 0.99 
Probability group 1 is lower than group 2:  less than 0.01

Reading It

  • prop.test(): employed minus not employed, about 15 to 21 points, p below 2.2e-16
  • bayes_prop_test(): 15.2 to 21.0 points, and more than a 99% probability the employed rate is higher
  • With 5,000 cases, the two traditions land on the same range

So a job gets you released? Not so fast. What else is different about these two groups?

The Groups Differ in Other Ways

checks counts how many police databases (prior arrests, convictions, parole, and so on) had the person’s name in them.

arrests |> summarize(avg_checks = mean(checks), people = n(), .by = employed)
# A tibble: 2 × 3
  employed avg_checks people
  <chr>         <dbl>  <int>
1 Yes            1.44   4111
2 No             2.35   1115

People without a job had more of a record, and a record is a reason to hold someone. The 18-point gap could be partly about records, not jobs.

Compare Like With Like

Keep only people with no record at all (checks == 0), and ask again.

arrests |> filter(checks == 0) |> count(employed, released)
# A tibble: 4 × 3
  employed released     n
  <chr>    <chr>    <int>
1 No       No          35
2 No       Yes        166
3 Yes      No         124
4 Yes      Yes       1526
  • Employed: 1,526 of 1,650 released (92.5%)
  • Not employed: 166 of 201 (82.6%)

Test Again: prop.test()

prop.test(x = c(1526, 166), n = c(1650, 201))

Test Again: prop.test()


    2-sample test for equality of proportions with continuity correction

data:  c(1526, 166) out of c(1650, 201)
X-squared = 21.112, df = 1, p-value = 4.333e-06
alternative hypothesis: two.sided
95 percent confidence interval:
 0.04224058 0.15571509
sample estimates:
   prop 1    prop 2 
0.9248485 0.8258706

Test Again: bayes_prop_test()

bayes_prop_test(x = c(1526, 166), n = c(1650, 201))

Test Again: bayes_prop_test()


    Bayesian test of two proportions 

data, group 1:  1,526 of 1,650 (92.5%)
data, group 2:  166 of 201 (82.6%)
prior:          know-nothing (1 yes, 1 no), same for both groups

                         estimate      95% credible interval
group 1                  92.4%         91.1% to 93.7%

Probability group 1 is lower than group 2:  less than 0.01

Where Is Zero?

Say It

  • Among people with clean records, the employed were released about 10 points more often: interval about 4 to 16 points (p = 0.000004), credible interval 5.1 to 15.8 points, and more than a 99% probability the employed rate is higher
  • The gap shrank from 18 points to 10 when we compared like with like, but it did not disappear
  • What this cannot tell you: whether some other difference (age, citizenship, where the arrest happened) explains the rest

filter() is the simplest honest move you have: make the groups more alike, then ask again.

Example 3 · Neighborhoods: Violence and Income

The Data

  • Chicago’s 77 community areas (our anchor data, 2025 crimes plus census numbers)
  • Group: the 38 areas with violent-crime rates above the city’s median vs the other 39
  • Outcome: median household income, a number in dollars
  • One row = one area, not one person

The outcome is a number, so: t.test().

Describe

areas |>
  summarize(avg_income = mean(median_income), areas = n(), .by = violence) |>
  arrange(violence)
# A tibble: 2 × 3
  violence      avg_income areas
  <fct>              <dbl> <int>
1 High violence     54355.    38
2 Other areas       90088.    39

See It

Each dot is one community area. Press Play.

Test

t.test(median_income ~ violence, data = areas)

Test


    Welch Two Sample t-test

data:  median_income by violence
t = -6.3561, df = 74.959, p-value = 1.458e-08
alternative hypothesis: true difference in means between group High violence and group Other areas is not equal to 0
95 percent confidence interval:
 -46932.44 -24533.58
sample estimates:
mean in group High violence   mean in group Other areas 
                   54354.63                    90087.64

Where Is Zero?

Say It

  • High-violence areas minus other areas: about $25,000 to $47,000 lower median income (averages $54,355 vs $90,088), p = 0.00000001
  • How big: the typical household in a high-violence area earns roughly $36,000 a year less. The unit is dollars because the column is dollars
  • What this cannot tell you:
    • which way it runs (does poverty breed violence, or does violence drive out income?) or what else explains both
    • anything about individual people. Each row is an area. “People with less income commit more violence” is a claim about people, and these data are not about people

Example 4 · Use of Force: Body Cameras Over Time

The Data

  • The Washington Post’s database of every person shot and killed by on-duty police in the U.S., 2015 to 2024: 10,430 people
  • body_camera is TRUE when news or police reports said a body camera recorded the incident
  • One row = one person killed

Question: after 2020’s wave of police reform, did body camera footage exist more often? Group: 2015 to 2019 vs 2020 to 2024. Yes/no outcome.

Count

shootings |> count(period, body_camera)
# A tibble: 4 × 3
  period       body_camera     n
  <chr>        <lgl>       <int>
1 2015 to 2019 FALSE        4329
2 2015 to 2019 TRUE          594
3 2020 to 2024 FALSE        4302
4 2020 to 2024 TRUE         1205
  • 2020 to 2024: 1,205 of 1,205 + 4,302 = 5,507 (21.9%)
  • 2015 to 2019: 594 of 594 + 4,329 = 4,923 (12.1%)

Year by Year

The share of fatal shootings with a body camera reported, one year at a time. Press Play.

What the Picture Says

  • The rise is real, but not smooth: 2023 hit 30%, and 2024 fell back to 20%
  • Comparing two five-year periods averages over those bumps. That is a choice, and an honest write-up says so

Now the two tests on the two periods.

Test: prop.test()

prop.test(x = c(1205, 594), n = c(5507, 4923))

Test: prop.test()


    2-sample test for equality of proportions with continuity correction

data:  c(1205, 594) out of c(5507, 4923)
X-squared = 174.76, df = 1, p-value < 2.2e-16
alternative hypothesis: two.sided
95 percent confidence interval:
 0.08374831 0.11256026
sample estimates:
   prop 1    prop 2 
0.2188124 0.1206581

Test: bayes_prop_test()

bayes_prop_test(x = c(1205, 594), n = c(5507, 4923))

Test: bayes_prop_test()


    Bayesian test of two proportions 

data, group 1:  1,205 of 5,507 (21.9%)
data, group 2:  594 of 4,923 (12.1%)
prior:          know-nothing (1 yes, 1 no), same for both groups

                         estimate      95% credible interval
group 1                  21.9%         20.8% to 23.0%
group 2                  12.1%         11.2% to 13.0%
difference (1 minus 2)   9.8 points    8.4 to 11.2 points

Probability group 1 is higher than group 2: more than 0.99 
Probability group 1 is lower than group 2:  less than 0.01

Where Is Zero?

Say It

  • prop.test(): about 8 to 11 points higher after 2020, p below 2.2e-16. bayes_prop_test(): 8.4 to 11.2 points, more than a 99% probability the later rate is higher
  • How big: about 12 in 100 fatal shootings had known camera footage before 2020, and about 22 in 100 after. Close to doubling, yet still fewer than 1 in 4
  • What this cannot tell you: whether cameras changed anything about the shootings, or how many cameras existed but were never reported

With 10,430 cases, the p-value was never in doubt. The size is the whole story.

Four Questions, One Recipe

Question Test Answer The lesson
Reentry Does aid lower re-arrest? prop.test() + Bayes 22% vs 31%; p = 0.063; 97% probably lower “Not significant” is not “no effect”
Pretrial Does a job get you released? prop.test() + Bayes, then again after filter() 18 points; 10 among clean records Groups differ in other ways. Compare like with like
Neighborhoods Lower income where violence is high? t.test() $25k to $47k lower Size in real units. Areas are not people
Use of force More body camera footage after 2020? prop.test() + Bayes 12% to 22% Huge n: the p-value adds nothing; the size is the story

How to Size Up a Dataset in Five Minutes

  1. What is one row? A person? An arrest? A neighborhood? A year?
  2. How many rows? Hundreds is plenty; millions is a headache
  3. What are the variables? glimpse() tells you names and types
  4. Anything strange? 999s, negative ages, lots of NA: summary() finds them
  5. Could it answer a question you care about? Name the groups, or the variables
