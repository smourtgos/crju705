A New Kind of Question

What Does Correlation Tell Us?

When two variables move together, how can we describe that relationship?

  • One goes up when the other goes up: positive correlation
  • One goes up when the other goes down: negative correlation
  • Changes in one don’t track changes in the other: no correlation

Direction is only half the story. The strength of the relationship tells how tightly the changes are linked. That’s the number we build today.

Correlation Does NOT Mean Causation

  • Correlation shows association, not cause and effect
  • Ice cream sales and violent crime rise together every year. Does ice cream cause violence?
  • Two variables can move together because of a third variable (summer heat drives both), or by pure coincidence
  • When you see a correlation, ask: does X cause Y? Y cause X? Does something else drive both? Or is it luck?
  • Correlation is a pattern, not proof of cause. Beware the lurking third variable!

From Covariance to r

Covariance: Moving Together, in Raw Units

Row by row: do x and y sit on the same side of their averages?

\[\text{cov}(x, y) \;=\; \frac{\sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{n - 1}\]

  • Both above average (or both below): positive products, positive covariance. Opposite sides: negative covariance
  • The sign is trustworthy: covariance tells you whether two variables move together
  • The size is not. Watch what happens on real data

The Units Problem

Two covariances with violent crime, from Chicago’s 77 community areas:

cov(areas$unemployment_rate, areas$violent_rate)
[1] 107.7543
cov(areas$median_income,     areas$violent_rate)
[1] -482660.1

Is -482,660 a “stronger” relationship than 108? No idea. The first number is in dollars times crimes per 1,000; the second in percentage points times crimes per 1,000. Covariance is glued to its units, so you can’t compare across pairs.

Correlation Rescales It

Divide the covariance by both standard deviations and the units cancel:

\[r \;=\; \frac{\text{cov}(x, y)}{s_x \, s_y}\]

cov(areas$unemployment_rate, areas$violent_rate) /
  (sd(areas$unemployment_rate) * sd(areas$violent_rate))
[1] 0.8398579
cor(areas$unemployment_rate, areas$violent_rate)   # same thing, one function
[1] 0.8398579

Always between -1 and +1, no units, comparable. Income runs r = -0.71 with violence, so unemployment (0.84) tracks violence more tightly than income does, direction aside.

Reading r

r (either sign) Strength What the scatterplot looks like
0.8 to 1.0 Very strong Dots hug a line
0.4 to 0.7 Moderate A clear trend, with plenty of scatter
0.1 to 0.3 Weak A slight tilt you might not see without the line
about 0 None (no straight-line pattern) A shapeless cloud

The sign gives the direction: + means both rise together; - means one rises as the other falls. These bands are conventions, not laws: in messy social-science data, a “moderate” r can be a major finding.

Calibrate Your Eye

By 0.5 (either sign) the trend is visible; below 0.2 your eye mostly sees noise, and it’s right.

What r Cannot See

Anscombe’s Quartet: Four Identical Summaries

anscombe_long <- anscombe |>
  pivot_longer(everything(), names_to = c(".value", "set"), names_pattern = "(.)(.)")

anscombe_long |>
  summarize(mean_x = mean(x), mean_y = mean(y), r = cor(x, y), .by = set)
# A tibble: 4 × 4
  set   mean_x mean_y     r
  <chr>  <dbl>  <dbl> <dbl>
1 1          9   7.50 0.816
2 2          9   7.50 0.816
3 3          9   7.5  0.816
4 4          9   7.50 0.817

Four little datasets that ship with R (Anscombe, 1973). Same averages, same r = 0.82. Statistically interchangeable. Right?

Anscombe’s Quartet, Seen

Only Set 1 is what you picture when someone says “r = 0.82.” Always plot before you correlate.

Limitation 1: r Only Sees Straight Lines

Think stress and performance: an upside-down U. r near 0 means no straight-line relationship, not no relationship.

Limitation 2: One Point Can Manufacture a Correlation

What Just Happened

  • Twenty points of pure noise happened to give r = -0.28. Small samples produce correlations out of nothing; that’s why we test, not just compute
  • Adding one extreme point swung r to +0.42: it flipped the sign and manufactured a “moderate positive” correlation
  • Neither number describes a real relationship
  • Outliers can also work the other way, hiding a real correlation
  • Defenses: plot first (you’d catch this instantly), and a correlation that doesn’t care about wild values…

A Rank-Based Backup: Spearman’s rho

Replace each value with its rank (1st, 2nd, 3rd…), then correlate the ranks. Spearman asks only: is the relationship consistently up, or consistently down?

c(pearson  = cor(outlier_data$x, outlier_data$y),
  spearman = cor(outlier_data$x, outlier_data$y, method = "spearman"))
   pearson   spearman 
 0.4235804 -0.1142857

To Spearman, the wild point is just “rank 21”: rho drifts from -0.29 to -0.11, still weak, while Pearson flipped sign. When data are skewed or have wild values, check Spearman alongside Pearson.

The Full Menu, in One Dataset

Strong positive, moderate positive, strong negative, and one genuine nothing (median age). Real data span the whole menu.

These Are Areas, Not People

Our r = 0.84 says: community areas with higher unemployment rates record more violent crime per 1,000 residents.

The ecological fallacy: using an association between groups (areas, states, cities) to make claims about individuals. Our data contain no individuals. Nothing here says unemployed people commit violence.

The classic example (Robinson, 1950): across U.S. states, more immigrants went with higher literacy. Across individuals, immigrants had lower literacy. Immigrants had settled in states with high literacy. Adding people up into areas can weaken, exaggerate, or even flip a relationship.

Saying It Out Loud (Area Data)

✅ Defensible:

“Community areas with higher unemployment rates recorded higher violent-crime rates (r = 0.84).”

❌ Not defensible:

“Unemployed residents commit more violent crime.” (A claim about individuals. These data can’t see individuals.)

“Unemployment drives up violence.” (A claim about cause. Concentrated poverty, disinvestment, and policing patterns all lurk behind both variables.)

Putting Uncertainty on r

From Description to a Test

We can see r = 0.84. The same question as every test since Session 6:

  • Null hypothesis: the true correlation is 0. No straight-line association in the process that produced these data
  • If it were 0, how surprising would an r like ours be, with 77 areas?
  • One wrinkle: these 77 areas are all of Chicago, not a sample. Read the null as a claim about the process that produces neighborhood outcomes, not about a bigger population we sampled from

cor.test()

cor.test(areas$unemployment_rate, areas$violent_rate)

    Pearson's product-moment correlation

data:  areas$unemployment_rate and areas$violent_rate
t = 13.4, df = 75, p-value < 2.2e-16
alternative hypothesis: true correlation is not equal to 0
95 percent confidence interval:
 0.7585749 0.8954023
sample estimates:
      cor 
0.8398579

Reading It

  • cor = 0.84 at the bottom: that’s r
  • 95% confidence interval: 0.76 to 0.90. An interval for the correlation, so it lives between -1 and +1. Where is zero? Far outside it
  • p-value < 2.2e-16: reject the null. A world with no correlation essentially never lines up 77 areas this tightly
  • With 77 areas, any r above about 0.22 (either sign) clears p < 0.05. Passing the test is a low bar; the strength is the story
  • Nothing in this output turns association into cause, or areas into individuals

When the Answer Is “Nothing Here”

 -0.2385791  0.2092774

Interval -0.24 to +0.21: zero is inside. Fail to reject. Say no evidence of a straight-line association, which is not proof there is none.

Several Pairs at Once

Real analyses rarely stop at one pair. cor() takes every numeric column you give it:

areas |>
  select(violent_rate, unemployment_rate, median_income, pct_vacant) |>
  cor() |>
  round(2)
                  violent_rate unemployment_rate median_income pct_vacant
violent_rate              1.00              0.84         -0.71       0.81
unemployment_rate         0.84              1.00         -0.81       0.67
median_income            -0.71             -0.81          1.00      -0.55
pct_vacant                0.81              0.67         -0.55       1.00

Read it like a mileage chart: each pair appears twice, and the 1s down the diagonal are each variable with itself. Handy for a first look at your project variables. Then plot and cor.test() the pairs that matter.

How to Report It

“Across Chicago’s 77 community areas, unemployment was strongly associated with the violent-crime rate, r = 0.84, 95% CI 0.76 to 0.90, p < 0.001. This is an area-level association; it does not speak to individuals.”

Direction, strength, uncertainty, and the level of analysis. Every time.

Part 2: Describe a Small Vector

days_held <- c(1, 1, 2, 2, 2, 3, 4, 20)
mean(days_held)
[1] 4.375
median(days_held)
[1] 2
sd(days_held)
[1] 6.390562
  • One long stay (20 days) pulls the mean up to 4.4, while the median stays at 2. Which one describes a typical stay? The median. The tail names the skew: this is right-skewed
  • The SD, in words: how far a typical value sits from the mean. Here, about 6 days, and most of that comes from one person

Part 3: The Counting Table

A risk tool flags people as “likely to reoffend.” 10% of people actually reoffend. The tool flags 80% of those who do, and 20% of those who don’t. Out of 1,000 people: of everyone flagged, what share actually reoffends?

Flagged Not flagged Total
Reoffends 80 20 100
Does not 180 720 900
Total 260 740 1,000

P(reoffends | flagged) = 80 / 260 = 0.31. The “80%” is P(flagged | reoffends), the other conditional. Read the bar out loud: whatever follows it is the denominator.

Part 4: Reading It
