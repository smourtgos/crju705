A Question to Start

If I told you the chance of being selected for a jury was 10%, and you weren’t selected after 10 tries, would that surprise you? Why or why not?

Two kinds of probability:

  • Theoretical probability — based on mathematical reasoning (a fair coin is 50/50 by definition)
  • Empirical probability — observed through repetition (flip it and count)

Today’s whole session is about the gap between these two — and how it shrinks.

Probabilities in the Long Run

Theoretical vs. Empirical Probability

Flip a fair coin 10 times:

flips_10 <- sample(c("heads", "tails"), size = 10, replace = TRUE)
table(flips_10) / 10
flips_10
heads tails 
  0.6   0.4

Now 10,000 times:

flips_10k <- sample(c("heads", "tails"), size = 10000, replace = TRUE)
table(flips_10k) / 10000
flips_10k
 heads  tails 
0.5055 0.4945

Empirical results fluctuate in small samples but converge toward the theoretical probability — the law of large numbers.

Watching the Long Run Arrive

lln <- tibble(
  flip = 1:10000,
  heads = sample(c(0, 1), size = 10000, replace = TRUE)
) |>
  mutate(running_prop = cumsum(heads) / flip)
ggplot(lln, aes(flip, running_prop)) +
  geom_line(color = crju_colors["navy"]) +
  geom_hline(yintercept = 0.5, color = "red", linetype = "dashed") +
  labs(title = "Proportion of heads in coin flips",
       x = "Number of flips", y = "Proportion heads")

Concept: in the long run, empirical ≈ theoretical. But “long run” might be longer than we think — look how wild the first few hundred flips are.

Simulating Probabilities in CJ (1)

False positive drug tests. Suppose a field test for drugs has a 5% false positive rate. Simulate 100 field tests of non-users:

tests <- sample(c("false positive", "correct"), size = 100,
                replace = TRUE, prob = c(0.05, 0.95))
table(tests)
tests
       correct false positive 
            91              9

Discussion: even with a low false positive rate, false positives occur regularly in practice. What do we think about this? Does it matter?

Simulating Probabilities in CJ (2)

Stop & frisk outcomes. Suppose 8% of stops yield contraband. What happens over 1,000 stops?

stop_outcomes <- sample(c("found", "nothing"), size = 1000,
                        replace = TRUE, prob = c(0.08, 0.92))
table(stop_outcomes)
stop_outcomes
  found nothing 
     69     931

Discussion: is that hit rate too low? What if each find were an illegal gun that would have been used in a violent crime? Simulation makes the long-run consequences of a policy concrete.

Random Does Not Mean Balanced

Two runs of 100 dice rolls — same die, same code:

dice <- tibble(
  run  = rep(c("Run 1", "Run 2"), each = 100),
  roll = c(sample(1:6, size = 100, replace = TRUE),
           sample(1:6, size = 100, replace = TRUE))
)
ggplot(dice, aes(x = factor(roll))) +
  geom_bar(fill = crju_colors["garnet"]) +
  facet_wrap(~run) +
  labs(title = "Dice roll frequencies — two runs of 100",
       x = "Face", y = "Count")

Does either look fair? Both are. Random does not mean balanced in small samples — remember this every time you see a “streak” in real data.

Random Does Not Mean Balanced

From One Sample to Many

A Population We Can Actually See

population-data.csv — 10,000 test scores. In real research we never see the whole population; today we get to cheat:

pop |>
  summarize(mean = mean(score), median = median(score),
            sd = sd(score), n = n())
# A tibble: 1 × 4
   mean median    sd     n
  <dbl>  <dbl> <dbl> <int>
1  100.   100.  15.0 10000

One Sample of 30

samp <- sample(pop$score, size = 30)
mean(samp)
[1] 100.3389
sd(samp)
[1] 13.16761

Close to the truth (mean ≈ 100, SD ≈ 15) — but not exact. That gap is sampling error, and it is not a mistake; it’s a fact of life.

The question that drives the rest of the semester: how wrong can one sample be?

What If We Sampled From the Tail?

Draw 10 scores from the bottom 5% of the population — and look at them in context:

tail_scores <- pop |> filter(score < quantile(score, 0.05))
tail_sample <- sample(tail_scores$score, size = 10)
mean(tail_sample)
[1] 70.02723

…And What If We Didn’t Know It?

Now pretend that sample is all we ever get to see:

If this were your dataset, you’d report a “typical” score near 70 — and you’d be badly wrong. How often does that actually happen with a random sample? Let’s find out.

Do It a Thousand Times

The key move of this whole session — instead of drawing one sample, draw 1,000 samples of 30 and keep each sample’s mean:

sample_means <- tibble(draw = 1:1000) |>
  mutate(mean_score = map_dbl(draw, \(d) mean(sample(pop$score, size = 30))))

sample_means
# A tibble: 1,000 × 2
   draw mean_score
  <int>      <dbl>
1     1       99.7
2     2       97.4
3     3       97.5
4     4       99.6
5     5      105. 
6     6      104.

The Sampling Distribution of the Mean

ggplot(sample_means, aes(mean_score)) +
  geom_histogram(bins = 40, fill = crju_colors["blue"], color = "white") +
  geom_vline(xintercept = mean(pop$score), color = "red", linewidth = 1.2) +
  labs(title = "1,000 sample means (n = 30 each)",
       x = "Sample mean", y = "Number of samples")

This histogram of means is a sampling distribution. It’s centered on the true population mean — and it’s much narrower than the population itself.

Two Distributions, Side by Side

Same center. Radically different spread. Individual scores wander all over; averages of 30 stay close to home.

The Standard Error

The SD of the sampling distribution has its own name: the standard error. And it has a formula — which our simulation can check:

sd(sample_means$mean_score)   # SE from the simulation
[1] 2.648695
sd(pop$score) / sqrt(30)      # SE from the formula: SD / sqrt(n)
[1] 2.733584

The standard error answers: “if I re-ran my study, how much would my estimate typically move?”

That single number is the foundation of confidence intervals and hypothesis tests — the rest of this course.

Sample Size Buys Precision

many_ns <- expand_grid(n = c(5, 30, 100), draw = 1:1000) |>
  mutate(mean_score = map_dbl(n, \(k) mean(sample(pop$score, size = k))))
ggplot(many_ns, aes(mean_score)) +
  geom_histogram(bins = 40, fill = crju_colors["navy"], color = "white") +
  geom_vline(xintercept = mean(pop$score), color = "red", linetype = "dashed") +
  facet_wrap(~n, labeller = label_both) +
  labs(x = "Sample mean", y = "Count")
many_ns |>
  summarize(simulated_se = sd(mean_score), formula_se = sd(pop$score) / sqrt(first(n)),
            .by = n)
# A tibble: 3 × 3
      n simulated_se formula_se
  <dbl>        <dbl>      <dbl>
1     5         6.84       6.70
2    30         2.77       2.73
3   100         1.49       1.50

The SE shrinks like 1/√n: to halve your uncertainty you need four times the data.

The Central Limit Theorem — With Real Crimes

The Least Normal Variable Imaginable

Back to our 30,000 real Chicago incidents. Whether an incident ended in arrest is as far from a bell curve as data gets — every value is TRUE or FALSE:

crimes |> count(arrest) |> mutate(prop = n / sum(n))
# A tibble: 2 × 3
  arrest     n  prop
  <lgl>  <int> <dbl>
1 FALSE  25176 0.839
2 TRUE    4824 0.161

Treat these 30,000 incidents as the population. True arrest proportion: 0.161.

Sample 100 Incidents. Repeat 1,000 Times.

Imagine an analyst who can only pull 100 case files at a time:

arrest_props <- tibble(draw = 1:1000) |>
  mutate(prop_arrest = map_dbl(draw, \(d) mean(sample(crimes$arrest, size = 100))))
ggplot(arrest_props, aes(prop_arrest)) +
  geom_histogram(binwidth = 0.01, fill = crju_colors["blue"], color = "white") +
  geom_vline(xintercept = mean(crimes$arrest), color = "red", linewidth = 1.2) +
  labs(title = "1,000 sample arrest proportions (n = 100 incidents each)",
       x = "Proportion of sampled incidents with an arrest", y = "Samples")

A Bell Curve Out of TRUE/FALSE Data

That’s the central limit theorem:

Whatever shape the population has — even a two-bar TRUE/FALSE stack — the sampling distribution of a mean (or proportion) becomes approximately normal as n grows, centered on the true value.

  • It’s why polls of ~1,000 people work for a country of 330 million
  • It’s why we can attach a standard error to an arrest rate, a clearance rate, a recidivism rate — without ever seeing the full population
  • It’s why the normal distribution keeps showing up even though raw CJ data almost never look normal

The CLT Also Obeys the √n Rule

At n = 25 a sample could easily tell you the arrest rate is 8% or 28%. At n = 400 you’re rarely off by more than a couple of points.

The n − 1 Payoff

Session 2’s IOU

In Session 2 we noticed var() and sd() divide by n − 1, not n, and I promised you’d see why. Simulation pays the debt. Draw 5,000 tiny samples (n = 5) and compute the variance both ways:

var_pop <- mean((pop$score - mean(pop$score))^2)   # true population variance

variance_sims <- tibble(draw = 1:5000) |>
  mutate(
    s            = map(draw, \(d) sample(pop$score, size = 5)),
    var_div_n    = map_dbl(s, \(x) sum((x - mean(x))^2) / length(x)),
    var_div_nm1  = map_dbl(s, \(x) var(x))          # R's built-in: n - 1
  )

Divide by n, and You’re Biased

variance_sims |>
  summarize(
    true_variance   = var_pop,
    avg_divide_by_n = mean(var_div_n),
    avg_divide_by_n_minus_1 = mean(var_div_nm1)
  )
# A tibble: 1 × 3
  true_variance avg_divide_by_n avg_divide_by_n_minus_1
          <dbl>           <dbl>                   <dbl>
1          224.            179.                    224.

Divide by n, and You’re Biased (cont.)

  • Dividing by n systematically underestimates the population variance — across 5,000 samples, not occasionally
  • Why? A sample clusters around its own mean, which is always closer to the sample’s values than the true mean is — so measured spread comes up short
  • Dividing by n − 1 inflates each estimate just enough to land, on average, on the truth. That’s all “unbiased estimator” means

What If the Population Is Unknown?

In real life you never get the left-hand panel. One draw from a mystery population:

unknown_population <- rnorm(1000, mean = 100, sd = 50)   # don't peek!
mystery_sample <- sample(unknown_population, size = 30)
mean(mystery_sample)
[1] 95.44168

Based on 30 points, you might assume normality — or skew, or outliers — and be wrong. Sampling-distribution logic is what lets us say how wrong we’re likely to be.

Why This Matters in CJ

  • Almost every number in CJ research is a sample statistic: a survey of officers, a sample of case files, one year of one city’s incidents
  • Every one of those statistics has a sampling distribution — it would come out differently if the study were re-run
  • The standard error turns “it would come out differently” into a number
  • Starting next session: use the SE to build confidence intervals — a range of values the truth is likely to be in
