The Logic of Inference

What Does “Inference” Mean?

Inference: a reasoning process that begins with some information and leads to some conclusion.

Inductive inference: reasons from specific cases to the more general — a cat jumps from a tree and lands on its feet; you infer something about cats generally.

Statistical inference: dealing systematically with situations where we have uncertain or incomplete information. Conclusions drawn inductively from samples are never fixed or firm.

You cannot PROVE anything from samples or by using statistical inference.

If We Can’t Prove Anything, What Are We Doing?

We collect data and analyze it with inferential statistics to build up a weight of evidence that influences our certainty about one conclusion or another.

Let’s Look at the Classic Example

  • 1854 — cholera breaks out in Soho, London
  • The debate: bad air (“miasma”) or contaminated water?
  • John Snow couldn’t test every household in London — that’s the whole population
  • So he mapped the sample of evidence he could get: deaths, and where people got their water

Sampled Evidence: Deaths and Pumps

deaths <- as_tibble(Snow.deaths) |> mutate(did = row_number())
pumps  <- as_tibble(Snow.pumps)  |> mutate(pid = row_number())

# Every death-pump distance, then each death's NEAREST pump
pairs <- crossing(did = deaths$did, pid = pumps$pid) |>
  left_join(deaths |> select(did, dx = x, dy = y), by = "did") |>
  left_join(pumps  |> select(pid, px = x, py = y), by = "pid") |>
  mutate(dist = sqrt((dx - px)^2 + (dy - py)^2))

nearest <- pairs |>
  slice_min(dist, n = 1, by = did)

by_pump <- nearest |>
  count(pid, name = "deaths_nearest") |>
  arrange(desc(deaths_nearest))

Why This Is Statistical Inference

  • Why wasn’t Snow just describing the map?
  • What bigger claim was he making about the whole city — and about cholera itself?

That jump — from the data you have to the world you don’t — is what we mean by inference.

Review from previous weeks — why do we sample?

We almost never have access to the totality of the population data. At its best, a sample is representative of a population.

Point Estimates and Their Discontents

A Rare Luxury: We Know the Truth

Our Chicago file holds ~30,000 incidents from 2025. Today, pretend the file is the whole population — then we can check our inferences against the truth.

crimes |> summarize(pop_arrest_rate = mean(arrest), n = n())
# A tibble: 1 × 2
  pop_arrest_rate     n
            <dbl> <int>
1           0.161 30000

The “true” citywide arrest proportion is 16.1%. Lock that in — in real research you never get to see this number.

Now Act Like a Researcher: Draw One Sample

A researcher can rarely afford all 30,000 case files. Suppose we pull a random sample of 250 incidents:

sample_250 <- crimes |> slice_sample(n = 250)

sample_250 |> summarize(sample_arrest_rate = mean(arrest))
# A tibble: 1 × 1
  sample_arrest_rate
               <dbl>
1              0.148

This is a point estimate — the sample’s best single guess at the population value.

14.8% vs the true 16.1%. Close-ish. But how good is this estimate? How certain are we?

Caution! Samples Fluctuate

Draw a fresh sample of 250 and the estimate moves. Do it 1,000 times:

many_estimates <- map_dbl(1:1000, \(i) {
  crimes |> slice_sample(n = 250) |> summarize(p = mean(arrest)) |> pull(p)
})

ggplot(tibble(p = many_estimates), aes(p)) +
  geom_histogram(bins = 30, fill = crju_colors["blue"], color = "white") +
  geom_vline(xintercept = mean(crimes$arrest),
             color = crju_colors["garnet"], linewidth = 1.2) +
  labs(title = "1,000 sample estimates of the arrest rate (n = 250 each)",
       subtitle = "Garnet line = the true population value",
       x = "Sample arrest proportion", y = "Number of samples")

Caution! Samples Fluctuate

What the Fluctuation Tells Us

quantile(many_estimates, c(0.025, 0.975))   # what is this telling us?
 2.5% 97.5% 
0.120 0.208
  • 95% of sample estimates landed between about 12% and 21% — that’s the sampling distribution from Session 4, live
  • Any single sample’s estimate could easily be a couple points off the truth
  • So reporting “14.8%” alone is overconfident — we owe our reader a range that reflects the fluctuation
  • Problem: this required 1,000 samples and a known population. A real researcher has one sample. Now what?

The Confidence Interval

Our First Inferential Tool

The confidence interval turns one sample into an honest range, using what Session 4 taught us about how sample statistics behave:

\[\text{estimate} \; \pm \; \underbrace{1.96 \times SE}_{\text{margin of error}}\]

  • SE (standard error) = the standard deviation of the sampling distribution, estimated from the one sample you have
  • 1.96 comes from the normal distribution: ±1.96 SDs covers the middle 95%

Bigger samples → smaller SE → narrower interval.

R Does This For Us: prop.test()

prop.test(sum(sample_250$arrest), n = 250)

    1-sample proportions test with continuity correction

data:  sum(sample_250$arrest) out of 250, null probability 0.5
X-squared = 122.5, df = 1, p-value < 0.00000000000000022
alternative hypothesis: true p is not equal to 0.5
95 percent confidence interval:
 0.1075899 0.1995409
sample estimates:
    p 
0.148

Our one sample of 250 says: point estimate 14.8%, 95% CI 10.8% to 20.0%. The true value (16.1%) is inside — this time. But “95% confident” of what, exactly?

The Classic Picture: 100 Samples, 100 Intervals

ci_100 <- map(1:100, \(i) {
  s  <- crimes |> slice_sample(n = 250)
  ci <- prop.test(sum(s$arrest), n = 250)$conf.int
  tibble(sample = i, estimate = mean(s$arrest), lo = ci[1], hi = ci[2])
}) |>
  list_rbind() |>
  mutate(captured = lo <= mean(crimes$arrest) & mean(crimes$arrest) <= hi)

sum(ci_100$captured)   # how many of the 100 intervals caught the truth?

The Classic Picture: 100 Samples, 100 Intervals

[1] 97
ggplot(ci_100, aes(sample, estimate, color = captured)) +
  geom_hline(yintercept = mean(crimes$arrest), color = "grey30", linewidth = 1) +
  geom_pointrange(aes(ymin = lo, ymax = hi), linewidth = 0.7, size = 0.25) +
  scale_color_manual(values = c("TRUE" = "grey65", "FALSE" = "#73000A")) +
  labs(title = paste0("100 samples, 100 confidence intervals — ",
                      sum(ci_100$captured), " capture the truth"),
       subtitle = "Horizontal line = true population arrest rate; garnet = intervals that missed",
       x = "Sample number", y = "Arrest proportion", color = "Captured?")

What Just Happened

  • Each of the 100 samples produced a different interval — the interval is a random quantity, the population value is fixed
  • 97 of our 100 intervals captured the true arrest rate; 3 missed (the garnet ones)
  • In the long run, the 95% procedure captures the truth in ~95% of replications — that long-run capture rate is the entire meaning of “95% confident”
  • The researchers who drew the garnet samples did nothing wrong — and their data give no warning that theirs are the unlucky intervals

In Practice

If we reran our whole study many times — drawing a new sample each time and constructing a new confidence interval each time — in 95% of those replications the interval would contain the true population value.

IMPORTANT:

  1. Our 95% CI does not necessarily contain the true population value
  2. Our 95% CI is not a statement about the probability that this particular interval is correct
  3. It is a long-run prediction based on numerous replications

WTF?!? — yes, everyone reacts this way. Point at the 100-intervals plot until it clicks.

Saying It Out Loud

✅ Defensible:

“We estimate the citywide arrest rate at 14.8% (95% CI: 10.8% to 20.0%). Values in this range are consistent with our data.”

“The method we used captures the true value 95% of the time.”

Saying It Out Loud: What Not to Say

❌ Not defensible:

“There is a 95% probability the true arrest rate is between 10.8% and 20.0%.” (The truth is fixed — it’s either in there or it isn’t.)

“95% of arrest rates fall in this interval.” (The CI is about the population parameter, not individual observations.)

Sample Size Buys Precision

Same procedure, three sample sizes, one draw each:

map(c(100, 250, 1000, 4000), \(n) {
  s  <- crimes |> slice_sample(n = n)
  ci <- prop.test(sum(s$arrest), n = n)$conf.int
  tibble(n = n, estimate = mean(s$arrest),
         lo = ci[1], hi = ci[2], width = ci[2] - ci[1])
}) |>
  list_rbind()
# A tibble: 4 × 5
      n estimate    lo    hi  width
  <dbl>    <dbl> <dbl> <dbl>  <dbl>
1   100    0.22  0.146 0.316 0.170 
2   250    0.192 0.146 0.247 0.101 
3  1000    0.154 0.132 0.178 0.0457
4  4000    0.163 0.152 0.175 0.0231

Quadrupling n roughly halves the width — precision grows with \(\sqrt{n}\), so it gets expensive fast.

Comparing Two Groups

A Real Question: Do Violent Crimes Happen Later in the Day?

Street lore says violence is a nighttime phenomenon. Property crime, the daytime hustle. Our data:

vp <- crimes |> filter(crime_category %in% c("Violent", "Property"))

vp |> summarize(mean_hour = mean(hour), sd_hour = sd(hour),
                n = n(), .by = crime_category)
# A tibble: 2 × 4
  crime_category mean_hour sd_hour     n
  <chr>              <dbl>   <dbl> <int>
1 Violent             12.8    6.87  9148
2 Property            12.5    6.63 10371

Two point estimates: violent crimes average hour 12.8, property 12.5. Is that ~15-minute gap real, or sample fluctuation?

Let’s Look at It Visually

ggplot(vp, aes(crime_category, hour, fill = crime_category)) +
  geom_boxplot(show.legend = FALSE) +
  scale_fill_manual(values = unname(crju_colors[c("blue", "garnet")])) +
  labs(x = NULL, y = "Hour of day (0–23)")

The boxes nearly coincide — the eye can’t settle this one. We need inference.

Resample the Difference

Same trick as before — resample each group, compute the difference in means, repeat:

violent_hours  <- vp$hour[vp$crime_category == "Violent"]
property_hours <- vp$hour[vp$crime_category == "Property"]

mean_diffs <- replicate(1000, {
  mean(sample(violent_hours,  length(violent_hours),  replace = TRUE)) -
  mean(sample(property_hours, length(property_hours), replace = TRUE))
})

quantile(mean_diffs, c(0.025, 0.975))   # what is this telling us?

The Two-Sample t-Test Gives the CI Directly

t.test(hour ~ crime_category, data = vp)

    Welch Two Sample t-test

data:  hour by crime_category
t = -2.6061, df = 19021, p-value = 0.009164
alternative hypothesis: true difference in means between group Property and group Violent is not equal to 0
95 percent confidence interval:
 -0.44272107 -0.06263688
sample estimates:
mean in group Property  mean in group Violent 
              12.54334               12.79602

Watch the direction: R orders groups alphabetically, so this tests Property − Violent — the mirror image of our resampled Violent − Property differences. Same finding, flipped sign.

Reading That Output Honestly

t.test(hour ~ crime_category, data = vp)$conf.int
[1] -0.44272107 -0.06263688
attr(,"conf.level")
[1] 0.95
  • The 95% CI for the difference (Property − Violent) runs from −0.44 to −0.06 hours — it excludes zero, so the data are consistent with violent crimes genuinely averaging later
  • But look at the magnitude: the difference is somewhere between about 4 and 27 minutes. Detectable ≠ dramatic.

Reading That Output Honestly, Continued

  • With ~19,500 incidents, we can detect tiny differences. The CI hands you both facts at once: direction (probably real) and size (small). Never report the first without the second.
  • This is your vaccination against next week’s topic — a “significant” result can be substantively trivial
