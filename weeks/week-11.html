Session 11 · Multiple Linear Regression

Least squares · the best-fitting plane in 3D · R² · holding constant · VIF · Bayesian regression with brms · Project Checkpoint 3

📽️ Slides: Multiple Linear Regression · 🧪 Lab 11: Multiple Regression — Chicago’s 77 Areas, Then 500 Neighborhoods · 📝 Homework 11 + Project Checkpoint 3 · 🎬 Demo walkthrough · ✍️ Practice

Session 10’s correlation said that two variables travel together; regression answers the sharper question — how much the outcome changes per unit of a predictor, and with several predictors, how much each matters after accounting for the others. Least squares gets built from scratch on ten students’ study hours, then scaled up to the interactive 3D plane through Chicago’s 77 community areas, where you watch shared variance get reassigned as a second predictor enters. The humility rules ride along: adjusted associations still aren’t causal effects, and area-level patterns still aren’t people-level claims. Checkpoint 3 fits the first regression on your own project data — and if your outcome is a yes/no, Session 12 hands you the right tool.

Learning objectives

By the end of this week you can:

  • fit and interpret lm() with several predictors, reading each coefficient as “holding the others constant”
  • explain R² — and what happens to a coefficient when a correlated predictor joins the model
  • run the basic diagnostics: a residuals-vs-fitted plot read honestly, and VIF for multicollinearity
  • fit the Bayesian version with brm() and report a coefficient with the credible-interval sentence
  • state what a regression coefficient does — and doesn’t — license you to claim

Before class

  • Read: Stanton Ch. 8 (Linear Multiple Regression)
  • Install one new package before class: install.packages("car") — we use it for multicollinearity checks (VIF). (brms you already have from ANOVA week; bayesplot rode along with it. The usual reminder applies: the first brm() of a session pauses at “Compiling Stan program…” — that’s normal.)
  • Bring your laptop and your final-project dataset — Homework 11 contains Checkpoint 3, the first regression model on your own data

In class

  • From Session 10’s correlation to today’s sharper question: not do two variables move together, but how much does the outcome change per unit of a predictor — holding the others constant
  • Least squares from scratch: three candidate lines through ten students’ study hours and exam scores, a sum-of-squared-residuals ruler to judge them, and lm() finding the one line no other line beats — then the animated best-fit search, and R² (which, with one predictor, is exactly Session 10’s r, squared)
  • Two predictors = a plane in 3D — the interactive plotly plane you can grab and spin in the browser, regenerated live from code, plus the recorded guided tour
  • Why “holding constant” earns its keep: ice cream sales “significantly predict” drownings until temperature enters the model and the association evaporates — and an added-variable plot to see the partialing happen
  • Real-data thread: violent-crime rates across Chicago’s 77 community areas — unemployment alone (slope ~3.3, R² ≈ .71), then vacancy added (unemployment’s slope drops to ~2.1, R² ≈ .82: shared variance reassigned before your eyes), a third predictor that doesn’t earn its keep, and a units lesson (a −0.0006 coefficient that’s actually ~6 fewer violent crimes per 1,000 residents per $10,000 of income)
  • Diagnostics: the residuals-vs-fitted plot read honestly (ours fans out at high fitted values — we say so), and VIF for multicollinearity
  • The Bayesian version: same formula in brm(), posterior distributions for each coefficient visualized with bayesplot, and the credible-interval sentence the confidence interval never allowed — plus model reporting sentences in both traditions
  • Standing warnings, now attached to coefficients: adjusted associations are not causal effects, and area-level associations are not people-level claims (the ecological fallacy rides along)
  • Lab 11: the full workflow yourself — Chicago start to finish, then your own model on the Lab 3 neighborhoods (including the residual plot’s tell that a count outcome is starting to strain the linear model)
