Session 10 · Measures of Association: Correlation

Covariance → Pearson’s r · always plot first · cor.test() · Bayesian correlation with the correlation package · the ecological fallacy · Project Checkpoint 2

📽️ Slides: Associations Between Variables — Correlation · 🧪 Lab 10: Correlation — Chicago Areas, Then a Null Worth Reporting · 📝 Homework 10 + Project Checkpoint 2 · 🎬 Demo walkthrough · ✍️ Practice

Before the midterm, every test had the same shape: a categorical grouping variable and a numeric outcome. The second half of the course drops the groups — this week both variables are numeric, and the question becomes whether two measures move together. Covariance grows into Pearson’s r, inference runs in both traditions, and the real-data thread climbs to Chicago’s 77 community areas — which forces the semester’s standing warning, the ecological fallacy, out into the open. Correlation is the doorway to regression in Sessions 11–12, where your final project lives; Checkpoint 2 computes the first associations in your own data.

Learning objectives

By the end of this week you can:

  • interpret r’s sign and size — and explain why you always plot before you trust it (Anscombe’s quartet is the cautionary tale)
  • test a correlation in both traditions: cor.test(), then the correlation package’s Bayesian output (posterior, credible interval, Bayes factor)
  • explain the ecological fallacy in one sentence, and catch a claim that commits it
  • report a null association honestly, letting the Bayes factor say “there’s nothing here”

Before class

  • Read: Stanton Ch. 7 (Measures of Association)
  • Install one new package before class: install.packages("correlation"). It’s a quick install — no compiling this time — and it runs both our frequentist and Bayesian correlations this week.
  • Bring your laptop and your final-project dataset — Homework 10 contains Project Checkpoint 2, which computes the first associations in your data
  • No new R4DS reading

In class

  • A new kind of question: both variables numeric, no groups — do two measures move together? (The doorway to regression in Sessions 11–12.)
  • Association vs. causation: ice cream and violent crime, and the lurking third variable
  • Covariance — the right idea, glued to its units — and Pearson’s r, the rescaled version that always lands between −1 and +1
  • What r cannot see: Anscombe’s quartet (four datasets, identical r, wildly different shapes), curved relationships, and one outlier manufacturing a correlation out of noise — plus Spearman’s rho, the rank-based backup
  • Real-data thread moves to the anchor dataset’s second level (codebook): Chicago’s 77 community areas. Unemployment tracks violent-crime rates at r = 0.84; median age gives a genuine zero; income shows why Pearson and Spearman can disagree
  • The ecological fallacy, named and practiced: area-level associations license no claims about individuals — the codebook has been promising this conversation all semester
  • Inference on r, both traditions: cor.test() (t on n − 2 df, CI) and the correlation package — correlation matrices with Holm-adjusted p-values, then bayesian = TRUE for posterior medians, credible intervals, pd, and Bayes factors, including what the "medium" vs. "wide" prior actually changes
  • Lab 10: the full workflow yourself — Chicago areas, then the Lab 3 neighborhoods (real associations) and the Lab 6 officers, where the honest answer is a null and the Bayes factor lets you say so
