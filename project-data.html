Final-Project Data Options

Real criminal justice datasets that load with one line of R

Your final project needs a real criminal justice dataset. This page is a menu: eight datasets that are ready to use, each loadable with one line of R, plus a few places to look if none of them fits your question. Explore at least two this week (that is Homework 7), then choose one.

The rules.

  • Any dataset on this page is eligible, including the ones we worked in class in Session 7. Your research question and variables have to go beyond what we did in class.
  • The course datasets (the Chicago files, crash data, officers, prisoners, neighborhoods, cities, reentry) are not eligible. They are for practice.
  • Found something else? Great. Clear it with me before you commit, and prefer a single file that downloads directly (a CSV is best).
  • Whatever you choose must have numeric variables for regression (Sessions 10 to 12). Each entry below says how well it does on that.

Two ways to load a dataset

Every dataset below has a course copy you load the way you have loaded everything this semester. Run library(tidyverse) first, once per session:

library(tidyverse)
arrests <- read_csv("https://smourtgos.github.io/crju705/data/toronto-arrests.csv")

Several also live in an R package, which is where the course copy came from. That route needs a one-time install:

install.packages("carData")   # once, typed in the console, not in your script
library(carData)
data(Arrests)                 # now there is an object called Arrests

Same data either way. The course copy is simpler, so it is what the examples below use.

How to size up a dataset in five minutes

  1. What is one row? A person? An arrest? A stop? A state?
  2. How many rows? Hundreds is plenty; millions is a headache
  3. What are the variables? glimpse(your_data) gives every name and type
  4. Anything strange? summary(your_data) shows impossible values (999s, negative ages) and NAs
  5. Could it answer a question you care about? Name the two groups and the outcome, or the variables you would relate

Rossi reentry experiment

432 men released from Maryland state prisons in the 1970s, followed for one year. Half were randomly assigned to receive financial aid. We used it in Session 7 (aid vs. no aid).

  • Key variables: arrest (1 = arrested within the year), week (week of first arrest; 52 if never), fin (financial aid), age, prio (number of prior convictions), wexp (work experience before prison), mar (married), paro (released on parole), educ (education level, coded 2 to 6), race
  • Numbers for regression: age, prio, week, educ
  • Starter question: were men released on parole re-arrested less often than men released without it?
  • Quirks: the original has 52 weekly employment columns (emp1 to emp52); the course copy leaves them out. educ is a code, not years: 2 = 6th grade or less, 3 = 7th to 9th, 4 = 10th to 11th, 5 = 12th, 6 = some college.
rossi <- read_csv("https://smourtgos.github.io/crju705/data/rossi.csv")

Original: carData package, data(Rossi). Rossi, Berk & Lenihan (1980), Money, Work, and Crime.

Toronto arrests

5,226 arrests by Toronto police for possession of small amounts of marijuana, 1997 to 2002, from a Toronto Star investigation. We used it in Session 7 (employed vs. not).

  • Key variables: released (released with a summons instead of held for a bail hearing), employed, citizen, sex, age, year, checks (how many of six police databases, such as prior arrests or parole, had the person’s name), colour
  • Numbers for regression: age, checks, year
  • Starter question: were citizens released with a summons more often than non-citizens?
arrests <- read_csv("https://smourtgos.github.io/crju705/data/toronto-arrests.csv")

Original: carData package, data(Arrests).

Minneapolis police stops

Nearly every stop the Minneapolis Police Department made in 2017. We used it in Lab 7 (searches in suspicious-person vs. traffic stops).

  • Key variables: problem (suspicious or traffic), citationIssued, personSearch, vehicleSearch, gender, policePrecinct (1 to 5), neighborhood, date, MDC, race, preRace
  • Numbers for regression: almost none (location and date). Outcomes are yes/no, so this one points toward logistic regression with categorical predictors
  • Starter question: among people stopped, were men searched more often than women?
  • Quirks: personSearch, vehicleSearch, and gender were recorded only for stops entered from a squad car’s computer (MDC), so the 8,221 stops by officers on foot, bike, or horseback show NA for them. citationIssued is missing for about 63% of all stops, so be careful building a question on it. NA means “not recorded,” not “no.”
stops <- read_csv("https://smourtgos.github.io/crju705/data/mpls-stops.csv")

Original: carData package, data(MplsStops); the Minneapolis open data portal has more years.

Fatal police shootings

The Washington Post’s database of every person shot and killed by an on-duty police officer in the United States, 2015 through 2024. We used it in Session 7 and Lab 7.

  • Key variables: date, state, city, armed_with, threat_type, flee_status, was_mental_illness_related, body_camera, age, gender, race
  • Numbers for regression: age only, at the level of the person. (Counting shootings by state or year opens more doors, but that takes skills we have not covered yet.)
  • Starter question: was a body camera reported more often when the person was in a mental health crisis?
  • Quirks: each row is a person who died, and the file includes names. Treat it with care, and leave names out of anything you show. This one is not copied to the course site; it is read straight from the Post.
shootings <- read_csv("https://raw.githubusercontent.com/washingtonpost/data-police-shootings/master/v2/fatal-police-shootings-data.csv")

Source: github.com/washingtonpost/data-police-shootings. License CC BY-NC-SA 4.0.

COMPAS risk scores

People arrested in Broward County, Florida, in 2013 and 2014, scored by the COMPAS pretrial risk-assessment tool, then followed for two years by ProPublica. We used it in Lab 7 (High vs. Low labels).

  • Key variables: two_year_recid (1 = arrested again within two years), score_text (Low, Medium, High), age, sex, priors_count...15 (number of prior offenses), juv_fel_count, juv_misd_count, c_charge_degree (F = felony, M = misdemeanor), race
  • Numbers for regression: several: age, the prior-count columns, the juvenile counts, the decile score
  • Starter question: were people charged with a felony re-arrested more often than people charged with a misdemeanor?
  • Quirks: 53 columns, many of them dates and case numbers you will not need. The file has two columns named decile_score and two named priors_count, so R renames them (the New names: message). The file includes names; leave them out of anything you show. Read straight from ProPublica.
compas <- read_csv("https://raw.githubusercontent.com/propublica/compas-analysis/master/compas-scores-two-years.csv")

Source: github.com/propublica/compas-analysis and ProPublica’s 2016 article “Machine Bias.”

GSS 2024: crime and justice attitudes

The General Social Survey, a national survey of U.S. adults run since 1972. The course copy keeps the 2024 respondents and the questions about crime, courts, guns, and police, plus basic background questions.

  • Key variables:
    • fear: afraid to walk alone at night in your neighborhood?
    • cappun: death penalty for murder
    • gunlaw: require a police permit to buy a gun
    • owngun: gun in the home
    • courts: courts too harsh or not harsh enough
    • grass: legalize marijuana
    • natcrime: spending on halting rising crime
    • polhitok, polabuse, polattak: when is it OK for police to strike a citizen
    • background: sex, degree, region, partyid, polviews
  • Numbers for regression: age, educ (years of school), coninc (family income in constant dollars)
  • Starter question: are women more likely than men to be afraid to walk alone at night in their neighborhood?
  • Quirks: the GSS asks each question of only part of the sample (a “split ballot”), so most questions have NA for somewhere between a third and three-quarters of people. NA means “not asked” or “no answer,” not “no.” Ages 89 and older are all coded 89.
gss <- read_csv("https://smourtgos.github.io/crju705/data/gss-2024-cj.csv")

Source: NORC at the University of Chicago, gss.norc.org. The full 2024 file has 813 variables, if you want to look for more.

U.S. states, 1960

A classic criminology dataset on crime and punishment across 47 states in 1960 (Ehrlich, 1973).

  • Key variables: y (crime rate), So (1 = Southern state), Prob (probability of imprisonment), Time (average time served), Po1 (police spending), Ed (schooling), U1, U2 (unemployment), Ineq (income inequality), GDP, M (share of young men)
  • Numbers for regression: nearly everything; this one was built for regression
  • Starter question: did Southern states have higher crime rates than the rest?
  • Quirks: only 47 rows, and several columns were rescaled to convenient numbers (for example, Ed is years of schooling times 10). Read ?MASS::UScrime before you interpret a number.
uscrime <- read_csv("https://smourtgos.github.io/crju705/data/uscrime.csv")

Original: the MASS package, which comes with R (nothing to install): MASS::UScrime.

U.S. arrest rates, 1973

Arrests per 100,000 residents for murder, assault, and rape in each of the 50 states in 1973, plus the percent of each state’s population living in cities.

  • Key variables: state, Murder, Assault, Rape, UrbanPop
  • Numbers for regression: all four, but only 50 rows and four variables
  • Starter question: do more urban states have higher assault arrest rates? (Split UrbanPop into two groups now; in Session 10 you will be able to use it as a number.)
  • Quirks: small and old. Fine for learning, thin for a whole project unless you pair it with a clear question.
usarrests <- read_csv("https://smourtgos.github.io/crju705/data/usarrests.csv")

Original: built into R, no package needed: USArrests.

Finding your own

If your question needs something else, these are good places to look. Check with me before you commit, and prefer a single CSV that downloads directly.

  • City open data portals. Chicago, New York, Los Angeles, Dallas, and dozens of other cities post crime incidents, arrests, stops, and use-of-force reports. Search “[city name] open data police.”
  • The Stanford Open Policing Project (openpolicing.stanford.edu): traffic stops from dozens of states and cities. The files are large; pick one city.
  • NACJD at ICPSR (icpsr.umich.edu/web/NACJD): the national archive of criminal justice research data. Excellent, but many files come in SPSS or Stata formats and need a free account.
  • The Bureau of Justice Statistics (bjs.ojp.gov): national statistics on jails, prisons, victimization, and courts, mostly as summary tables.
