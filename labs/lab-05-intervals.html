Lab 5 · Putting an Interval On It

CRJU 705 · Session 5

Goal: turn today’s lecture into three lines of R you will use for the rest of your career — a point estimate, a confidence interval around it, and a sentence about that interval that is actually true. Two functions do all the work: prop.test() and t.test().

How labs work: run the Setup chunk as-is, follow the Walkthrough along with me, then complete the Your Turn tasks in your own script. The Exit Ticket gets submitted to Canvas before you leave.

Setup

Open RStudio, start a new R script, and run:

library(tidyverse)

officers <- read_csv("https://smourtgos.github.io/crju705/data/officers.csv",
                     show_col_types = FALSE) |>
  filter(!is.na(ptsd_screen))

officers holds survey records for correctional officers (simulated, but realistic): shift, tenure, rank, overtime, and three wellness measures — burnout, job_satisfaction, and sleep_hours — plus a ptsd_screen result.

That filter() line is doing something you should never do silently. Eight of the 200 officers have no PTSD screen on file, and sum() returns NA the moment a missing value touches it. We drop those eight and say so out loud, which leaves 192. What you should do about missing data is a real question, and not today’s.

officers |> count(ptsd_screen)
# A tibble: 2 × 2
  ptsd_screen     n
  <chr>       <int>
1 Negative      150
2 Positive       42

Walkthrough

A point estimate, and why it isn’t an answer

42 / 192
[1] 0.21875

About 22% of these officers screened positive. Now the honest question: we surveyed 192 officers, not every officer in the state. If someone else surveyed a different 192, they would not get 22%. Session 4 told us exactly how much that number moves around. This week we put that movement in the answer.

prop.test() does it for you

prop.test(sum(officers$ptsd_screen == "Positive"), n = nrow(officers))

    1-sample proportions test with continuity correction

data:  sum(officers$ptsd_screen == "Positive") out of nrow(officers), null probability 0.5
X-squared = 59.63, df = 1, p-value = 1.145e-14
alternative hypothesis: true p is not equal to 0.5
95 percent confidence interval:
 0.1637971 0.2852208
sample estimates:
      p 
0.21875

Ignore most of that output. R is testing whether the proportion equals 0.5, which is a question nobody asked. The line you want is the 95 percent confidence interval: roughly 0.16 to 0.29.

prop.test(sum(officers$ptsd_screen == "Positive"), n = nrow(officers))$conf.int
[1] 0.1637971 0.2852208
attr(,"conf.level")
[1] 0.95

Saying it out loud without lying

Here is the sentence I want:

In this sample of 192 correctional officers, about 22% screened positive for PTSD. Accounting for sampling, the plausible range is roughly 16% to 29%.

And here is what not to say:

  • “There is a 95% chance the true rate is between 16% and 29%.” The true rate is a fixed number. It is not doing anything probabilistic. The 95% describes the method, not this one interval.
  • “95% of officers fall between 16% and 29%.” The interval is about the rate, not about individual officers.
  • “The rate is 22%.” That is the estimate with the uncertainty deleted.

The width of that interval is the honest part. Do not throw it away.

Width is a choice you pay for

set.seed(705)
small <- slice_sample(officers, n = 50)

prop.test(sum(small$ptsd_screen == "Positive"), n = nrow(small))$conf.int
[1] 0.1050216 0.3414368
attr(,"conf.level")
[1] 0.95

Same population, a quarter of the sample, and the interval roughly doubles in width. That is the \(\sqrt{n}\) rule from last week, showing up in the thing you actually report.

Two groups, and an interval that contains zero

Do officers who screen positive sleep less?

officers |>
  summarize(mean_sleep = mean(sleep_hours), n = n(), .by = ptsd_screen)
# A tibble: 2 × 3
  ptsd_screen mean_sleep     n
  <chr>            <dbl> <int>
1 Negative          6.32   150
2 Positive          6.58    42
t.test(sleep_hours ~ ptsd_screen, data = officers)$conf.int
[1] -0.7721282  0.2568901
attr(,"conf.level")
[1] 0.95

Two things to notice. First, R subtracts alphabetically, so this is Negative minus Positive. Second, and this is the whole lesson: the interval runs from about -0.77 to +0.26 hours, and zero is inside it.

That does not mean the groups are the same. It means this sample cannot tell you which direction the difference runs. The honest sentence:

Officers screening positive slept about 15 minutes more per night on average, but with 192 officers the data are consistent with anything from roughly 15 minutes less to roughly 45 minutes more, including no difference at all.

Watch the direction there. R handed us the interval for Negative minus Positive, and my sentence talks about the Positive group, so the signs flip when I say it out loud. Getting that backwards is the easiest mistake in this whole lab, and I want you to see me being careful about it.

Learning to write that sentence, instead of “no effect” or “they sleep more,” is most of what this course is for.
