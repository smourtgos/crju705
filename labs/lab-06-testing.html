Lab 6 · One Question, Two Tests

CRJU 705 · Session 6

Goal: by the end of this lab you can take one yes/no question about two groups and answer it twice: with a p-value from prop.test(), and with a posterior from bayes_prop_test(). Same four numbers go into both. What changes is the sentence each one lets you say.

How labs work: run the Setup chunk as-is, follow the Walkthrough along with me, then complete the Your Turn tasks in your own script. That script is what you submit to Canvas before you leave.

Setup

Open RStudio, start a new R script, and run:

library(tidyverse)
source("https://smourtgos.github.io/crju705/R/bayes.R")

officers <- read_csv("https://smourtgos.github.io/crju705/data/officers.csv",
                     show_col_types = FALSE) |>
  filter(!is.na(ptsd_screen)) |>
  mutate(ptsd_screen = factor(ptsd_screen, levels = c("Positive", "Negative")))

These are last week’s correctional officers, set up exactly the same way: the eight officers with no PTSD screen on file are dropped (we say so out loud), which leaves 192, and Positive comes first.

One line is new. source() runs an R file from the course website, and that file gives you one new function, bayes_prop_test(). There is nothing to install. If you ever see could not find function "bayes_prop_test", you skipped this line.

Walkthrough

Count first

Our question: do male and female officers screen positive for PTSD at different rates? Every test today starts from a count, just like last week:

officers |> count(gender, ptsd_screen)
# A tibble: 6 × 3
  gender          ptsd_screen     n
  <chr>           <fct>       <int>
1 Female          Positive        8
2 Female          Negative       37
3 Male            Positive       33
4 Male            Negative      111
5 Nonbinary/Other Positive        1
6 Nonbinary/Other Negative        2

Read four numbers off that table, two per group: how many screened positive, out of how many.

  • Male: 33 positive, out of 33 + 111 = 144
  • Female: 8 positive, out of 8 + 37 = 45

(Three officers are listed as Nonbinary/Other. Three people is too few to compare with anyone, so we leave that row alone and say so.)

33 / 144
[1] 0.2291667
8 / 45
[1] 0.1777778

About 23% of the men and 18% of the women. A gap of about 5 percentage points. Male goes first all the way through, so every difference reads Male minus Female.

Is a 5-point gap real, or is it the kind of gap you get by luck with only 45 women in the sample?

The frequentist test: prop.test() with two groups

Last week you typed one “how many” and one “out of how many.” With two groups you type two of each, wrapped in c(). Male first, both times:

prop.test(x = c(33, 8), n = c(144, 45))

    2-sample test for equality of proportions with continuity correction

data:  c(33, 8) out of c(144, 45)
X-squared = 0.27341, df = 1, p-value = 0.6011
alternative hypothesis: two.sided
95 percent confidence interval:
 -0.09430734  0.19708512
sample estimates:
   prop 1    prop 2 
0.2291667 0.1777778

Read it from the bottom up.

  • prop 1 and prop 2 are the two rates, 0.229 and 0.178. Male is prop 1 because we typed it first.
  • 95 percent confidence interval is for the difference, Male minus Female: about -0.09 to +0.20, or 9 points lower to 20 points higher. Zero is inside it. That is last week’s skill.
  • p-value = 0.6011 is tonight’s new line. If men and women truly screened positive at the same rate, a gap this big or bigger would still turn up about 60% of the time, just from the luck of who landed in the sample.

A p-value of 0.60 is nowhere near 0.05. The verdict is “fail to reject the null.”

Saying it out loud without lying

Here is the sentence I want:

In this sample, 23% of male officers and 18% of female officers screened positive. A gap that size is well within what chance alone produces (p = 0.60), so these data cannot tell us whether the true rates differ.

And here is what not to say:

  • “There is a 60% chance that men and women have the same rate.” The p-value is about the data, assuming the null is true. It is never the probability that the null is true.
  • “We proved there is no difference.” Failing to reject is not proving. The interval allows anything from 9 points lower to 20 points higher.
  • “Men have higher PTSD rates (23% vs 18%).” That is the estimate with the uncertainty deleted.

The Bayesian test: same four numbers

Now hand the same four numbers to bayes_prop_test():

bayes_prop_test(x = c(33, 8), n = c(144, 45))

    Bayesian test of two proportions 

data, group 1:  33 of 144 (22.9%)
data, group 2:  8 of 45 (17.8%)
prior:          know-nothing (1 yes, 1 no), same for both groups

                         estimate      95% credible interval
group 1                  23.3%         16.8% to 30.5%
group 2                  19.1%         9.4% to 31.4%
difference (1 minus 2)   4.2 points    -9.6 to 16.4 points

Probability group 1 is higher than group 2: 0.74 
Probability group 1 is lower than group 2:  0.26

Read it top to bottom.

  • The first lines repeat your data back to you, so you can check your typing.
  • prior says “know-nothing.” We did not tell it what we believed beforehand, so it started from a blank slate.
  • estimate is the best single guess for each group’s true rate, and each group gets its own credible interval.
  • difference (1 minus 2) is Male minus Female: about 4 points, with a 95% credible interval of roughly -10 to +16 points.
  • The last two lines answer the question directly: the probability that the male rate is higher is about 0.74.

Here is the sentence this one lets you say, and it is the sentence we were forbidden to say about a confidence interval last week:

Given these data, there is a 95% probability that the true difference is between 10 points lower and 16 points higher for men, and about a 74% probability that the male rate is the higher one.

Notice the two tests do not disagree. Both intervals run from roughly -10 to somewhere between +16 and +20. One stops at “not significant.” The other tells you how probable each direction is: 74% is better than a coin flip, and a long way from sure.

Adding what you knew beforehand

A Bayesian can bring in earlier knowledge, as long as they say it out loud. Suppose earlier surveys at these facilities found about 1 officer in 5 screening positive, and you trust that about as much as you would trust having already seen 20 officers: 4 positive, 16 negative. That belief is two numbers:

bayes_prop_test(x = c(33, 8), n = c(144, 45), prior = c(4, 16))

    Bayesian test of two proportions 

data, group 1:  33 of 144 (22.9%)
data, group 2:  8 of 45 (17.8%)
prior:          4 yes, 16 no (about 20%, worth 20 cases), same for both groups

                         estimate      95% credible interval
group 1                  22.6%         16.5% to 29.2%
group 2                  18.5%         10.1% to 28.7%
difference (1 minus 2)   4.1 points    -7.6 to 15.0 points

Probability group 1 is higher than group 2: 0.77 
Probability group 1 is lower than group 2:  0.23

Look at the prior line, then at what changed: almost nothing. The probability moved from 0.74 to 0.77. With 189 real officers in the data, 20 imaginary ones do not carry much weight. A prior matters most when you have very little data.

The p-value was in t.test() the whole time

Last week you ran this line and read the interval. Look one line higher:

t.test(sleep_hours ~ ptsd_screen, data = officers)

    Welch Two Sample t-test

data:  sleep_hours by ptsd_screen
t = 1.0014, df = 60.499, p-value = 0.3206
alternative hypothesis: true difference in means between group Positive and group Negative is not equal to 0
95 percent confidence interval:
 -0.2568901  0.7721282
sample estimates:
mean in group Positive mean in group Negative 
              6.580952               6.323333

p-value = 0.3206. If officers who screen positive and negative truly slept the same amount, a gap as big as ours (about 15 minutes) would turn up about a third of the time by luck. Fail to reject. It is the same conclusion the interval gave you last week, because zero was inside it.
