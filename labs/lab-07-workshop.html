Lab 7 · Real Questions, Real Tests

CRJU 705 · Session 7

Goal: by the end of this lab you can take a real criminal justice dataset you have never seen, ask it a two-group question, and answer it with the same four steps every time. No new functions tonight. Everything here is something you already ran in Labs 5 and 6, pointed at data researchers actually use.

How labs work: run the Setup chunk as-is, follow the Walkthrough along with me, then complete the Your Turn tasks in your own script. That script is what you submit to Canvas before you leave.

The recipe

Every question tonight, in lecture and here, runs the same four steps:

  1. Question. Name the two groups and the outcome. If the outcome is yes/no, the test is prop.test() (and bayes_prop_test()). If the outcome is a number, the test is t.test().
  2. Count or describe. count() for a yes/no outcome; the group averages for a number.
  3. Test. Type the numbers in, or give t.test() the formula.
  4. Say it. The honest sentence, how big in real units, and one thing this test cannot tell you.

Three datasets, three read_csv() lines, the same line you have used all semester. Two of them come straight from the people who built them (ProPublica and the Washington Post), and one is a copy on our course site. Each mutate() line does what you did in Homework 5: it turns a 1/0 or TRUE/FALSE column into two labeled groups, with the group we care about first.

When the first file loads, R prints a message that starts New names:. That file has two columns called decile_score and two called priors_count, and R renamed the second copy of each so it can tell them apart. Nothing is broken, and there is nothing to fix. Real data does this.

Walkthrough

The data

In 2013 and 2014, Broward County, Florida, used a commercial tool called COMPAS to score people arrested and awaiting trial. Each person got a label: Low, Medium, or High risk. ProPublica then checked who was arrested again within two years. One row is one defendant. two_year_recid is 1 if that person was arrested again within two years.

Question

Do people the tool labels High risk reoffend more often than people it labels Low risk? Two groups (High, Low). A yes/no outcome (reoffended or not). So: prop.test(), then bayes_prop_test().

Count

compas |> count(score_text, two_year_recid)

  <chr>               <dbl> <int>
1 High                    0   402
2 High                    1  1001
3 Low                     0  2681
4 Low                     1  1216
5 Medium                  0   880
6 Medium                  1  1034

Four numbers, two per group, High first:

  • High: 1,001 reoffended, out of 1,001 + 402 = 1,403
  • Low: 1,216 reoffended, out of 1,216 + 2,681 = 3,897

We are comparing only High and Low tonight, so the Medium rows sit out. Say so out loud whenever you leave a group aside.

1001 / 1403
[1] 0.7134711
1216 / 3897
[1] 0.3120349

About 71% of people labeled High were arrested again, against about 31% of people labeled Low.

Test

prop.test(x = c(1001, 1216), n = c(1403, 3897))

    2-sample test for equality of proportions with continuity correction

data:  c(1001, 1216) out of c(1403, 3897)
X-squared = 681.57, df = 1, p-value < 2.2e-16
alternative hypothesis: two.sided
95 percent confidence interval:
 0.3731784 0.4296940
sample estimates:
   prop 1    prop 2 
0.7134711 0.3120349

High minus Low: the interval runs from about 37 to 43 points, and the p-value is below 2.2e-16. Reject the null.

bayes_prop_test(x = c(1001, 1216), n = c(1403, 3897))

    Bayesian test of two proportions 

data, group 1:  1,001 of 1,403 (71.3%)
data, group 2:  1,216 of 3,897 (31.2%)
prior:          know-nothing (1 yes, 1 no), same for both groups

                         estimate      95% credible interval
group 1                  71.3%         68.9% to 73.7%
group 2                  31.2%         29.8% to 32.7%
difference (1 minus 2)   40.1 points   37.3 to 42.9 points

Probability group 1 is higher than group 2: more than 0.99 
Probability group 1 is lower than group 2:  less than 0.01

Credible interval about 37 to 43 points, and more than a 99% probability that the High group’s rate is the higher one. With 5,300 people, the two traditions land on the same range.

Say it

  • The sentence: given these data, there is a 95% probability that the true gap in two-year re-arrest between people labeled High and Low is between about 37 and 43 percentage points.
  • How big, in real units: about 71 of every 100 people labeled High were arrested again within two years, compared with about 31 of every 100 labeled Low.
  • What this test cannot tell you: whether the tool is good enough to decide who stays in jail. Almost 3 in 10 people labeled High were not arrested again, and “arrested again” is not the same as “committed a crime.” A p-value cannot answer whether that error rate is acceptable. That is a question about values, not statistics.

Same data, a number instead of yes/no

Were the people who reoffended younger? The outcome is now age, a number, so the test is t.test(). The reoffended column was set up in Setup with Reoffended first.

t.test(age ~ reoffended, data = compas)

    Welch Two Sample t-test

data:  age by reoffended
t = -16.7, df = 7192.1, p-value < 2.2e-16
alternative hypothesis: true difference in means between group Reoffended and group Did not is not equal to 0
95 percent confidence interval:
 -5.081144 -4.013598
sample estimates:
mean in group Reoffended    mean in group Did not 
                32.31990                 36.86727

Reoffended minus Did not: about 4 to 5 years younger (32.3 vs 36.9 on average), p below 2.2e-16.

In plain words: people who were arrested again were, on average, about four and a half years younger. That is one of the oldest findings in criminology (offending peaks young), showing up again in a dataset built for something else.
