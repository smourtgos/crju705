Lab 3 · Data Transformation with dplyr

CRJU 705 · Session 3

Goal: learn the five dplyr verbs you’ll use in nearly every analysis this semester — filter(), arrange(), mutate(), summarize(), and count() — and use them to compute the probabilities from today’s lecture on real-looking neighborhood data. This lab assumes you’ve read R4DS Ch. 3 (Data transformation) and Ch. 4 (Workflow: code style).

How labs work: run the Setup chunk as-is, follow the Walkthrough along with me, then complete the Your Turn tasks in your own script. The Exit Ticket gets submitted to Canvas before you leave.

mutate() — make new columns

mutate() adds columns computed from existing ones:

nbhd <- nbhd |>
  mutate(
    poverty_pct   = poverty_rate * 100,
    high_disorder = disorder_level %in% c("High", "Very High")
  )

nbhd |> select(nbhd_id, poverty_pct, disorder_level, high_disorder) |> head(4)
# A tibble: 4 × 4
  nbhd_id poverty_pct disorder_level high_disorder
    <dbl>       <dbl> <chr>          <lgl>        
1       1        8.41 Moderate       FALSE        
2       2       37.8  Very High      TRUE         
3       3       25.4  Moderate       FALSE        
4       4       27.0  Moderate       FALSE

Note we assigned the result back to nbhd — pipelines don’t change the data unless you save them.

summarize() with .by — one row per group

summarize() collapses many rows into summaries; .by = does it per group:

nbhd |>
  summarize(
    mean_assaults = mean(assaults),
    mean_poverty  = mean(poverty_rate),
    n             = n(),
    .by = disorder_level
  )
# A tibble: 4 × 4
  disorder_level mean_assaults mean_poverty     n
  <chr>                  <dbl>        <dbl> <int>
1 Moderate               1.04         0.273   255
2 Very High              2.21         0.534    24
3 Low                    0.528        0.143   127
4 High                   1.62         0.410    94

Assaults and poverty both climb with observed disorder — one pipeline, four verbs of vocabulary from lecture.

count() — the fast path to contingency tables

count() is a shortcut for grouped counting, and it’s how we built every probability in lecture:

nbhd |> count(disorder_level)
# A tibble: 4 × 2
  disorder_level     n
  <chr>          <int>
1 High              94
2 Low              127
3 Moderate         255
4 Very High         24

Two variables gives the long form of a contingency table; pivot_wider() makes it look like the lecture tables:

nbhd |>
  count(historic, high_disorder) |>
  pivot_wider(names_from = high_disorder, values_from = n)
# A tibble: 2 × 3
  historic `FALSE` `TRUE`
  <chr>      <int>  <int>
1 No           303     99
2 Yes           79     19

And dividing by sum(n) turns counts into probabilities. Marginal:

nbhd |> count(high_disorder) |> mutate(p = n / sum(n))
# A tibble: 2 × 3
  high_disorder     n     p
  <lgl>         <int> <dbl>
1 FALSE           382 0.764
2 TRUE            118 0.236

Conditional — filter to the condition, then take the proportion (the lecture’s renormalization move):

nbhd |>
  filter(historic == "Yes") |>
  summarize(p_high_given_historic = mean(high_disorder))
# A tibble: 1 × 1
  p_high_given_historic
                  <dbl>
1                 0.194

Compare that to the marginal P(high disorder) above — does knowing a neighborhood is historic change the probability much?

Code style — 30 seconds that save hours

From R4DS Ch. 4, the habits I will nag you about all semester:

  • Spaces around <-, =, and after commas: mean_x = mean(x), not mean_x=mean(x)
  • After a pipe |>, put the next verb on a new line, indented two spaces
  • Names: lowercase, words separated by _ (high_disorder, not HighDisorder or hd2)
  • Comments explain why, code shows what

Your future self is your most frequent collaborator. Be kind to them.
