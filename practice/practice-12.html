Practice Problems · Session 11

Logistic regression: odds, odds ratios, and predicted probabilities

Ungraded self-checks. Work each problem in your own R session before clicking the solution. Homework 11 is your own final-project model, so this bank is where you drill the logistic workflow on neutral data first: if you can do these cold, Checkpoint 4 (and the presentation questions afterward) will feel routine.

Problems that use data need only this setup: the 20,000 Alaska crashes from Lab 4, Homeworks 5 and 6, and Lab 11. The question is the lab’s: does an intoxicated driver make a crash more likely to injure someone? Here the model is leaner (two predictors instead of the lab’s three), so the numbers are close to the lab’s but not identical. The outcome is the flag you have built before:

library(tidyverse)

crashes <- read_csv("https://smourtgos.github.io/crju705/data/crash-ak.csv") |>
  mutate(injury = injuries > 0)   # TRUE = someone was injured

Problem 1 · Odds ↔︎ probability, three conversions

No data, just the conversions. (a) About 12% of released defendants in a jurisdiction fail to appear: convert that probability to odds, and phrase it as “1 to ___.” (b) A program director says the odds a participant completes supervision are 4: what probability is that? (c) A logistic model reports a baseline log-odds of -1.99: use plogis() to recover the probability, and notice where you’ve landed.

Tip

Solution

0.12 / (1 - 0.12)   # (a) probability -> odds
[1] 0.1363636
4 / (4 + 1)         # (b) odds -> probability
[1] 0.8
plogis(-1.99)       # (c) log-odds -> probability
[1] 0.1202569
qlogis(0.12)        # and back: probability -> log-odds
[1] -1.99243
  1. Odds ≈ 0.136, about 1 to 7.3: for every failure to appear, roughly seven appearances. (b) Odds of 4 (“four to one”) is a probability of 0.80. (c) plogis(-1.99) ≈ 0.12: you’ve come back to (a), because -1.99 is exactly qlogis(0.12). Probability, odds, and log-odds are three readouts of one quantity; plogis() and qlogis() move you between them, and the model does its arithmetic on the log-odds scale.

Problem 2 · Fit it: glm(family = binomial)

Sign and significance are readable right off this table; the sizes are not, because nobody thinks in log-odds, which is exactly why the next move is always exp().

Problem 3 · Exponentiate: log-odds → odds ratios

Turn the model into odds ratios and their confidence intervals (exp(coef(...)), then exp(confint(...))). Interpret each predictor in one sentence with the holding-constant clause, and state what the “no effect” value is for an odds ratio.

Tip

Solution

exp(coef(injury_model))
 (Intercept)    intox_any num_vehicles 
    0.568485     1.912470     1.267901 
exp(confint(injury_model))
                 2.5 %    97.5 %
(Intercept)  0.4994012 0.6468929
intox_any    1.7188884 2.1296050
num_vehicles 1.1823033 1.3598917
  • Intoxication: OR ≈ 1.91 [1.72, 2.13]; a crash involving an intoxicated driver has about 91% higher odds of injuring someone than a sober-driver crash, holding the number of vehicles constant.
  • Vehicles: OR ≈ 1.27 [1.18, 1.36]; each additional vehicle increases the odds of injury by about 27%. (In these data num_vehicles is only ever 1 or 2, so this is really the two-vehicle vs. single-vehicle contrast: check your predictor’s actual values before narrating a “per unit” story.)
  • For odds ratios the no-effect value is 1, not 0: both intervals sit clearly above it.

exp() converts every log-odds coefficient into a percent change in the odds, and 1 is the number that means “nothing happening.”

Problem 4 · From effects to cases: predicted probabilities

Odds ratios describe effects; a briefing needs cases. Use predict(type = "response") for two crash profiles ((i) sober driver, single vehicle; (ii) intoxicated driver, two vehicles). How do the two probabilities compare with the 48% baseline?

Tip

Solution

profiles <- tibble(intox_any = c(0, 1), num_vehicles = c(1, 2))

profiles |>
  mutate(p_injury = predict(injury_model, newdata = profiles, type = "response"))
# A tibble: 2 × 3
  intox_any num_vehicles p_injury
      <dbl>        <dbl>    <dbl>
1         0            1    0.419
2         1            2    0.636

A sober single-vehicle crash carries about a 42% predicted probability of injury; an intoxicated two-vehicle crash about 64%: one below the 48% baseline, one well above it. Report odds ratios for effects and predicted probabilities for cases: decision-makers think in probabilities, and predict() is how you hand them one.

Problem 5 · Audit the memo (the classic mistake)

A draft memo about your model reads:

“The odds ratio for intoxication is 1.91, so a crash with an intoxicated driver is 91% more likely to injure someone. Since about 48% of crashes cause injury, that works out to roughly 0.48 × 1.91 ≈ 92% of intoxicated-driver crashes causing injury.”

Both bolded claims are wrong, and the second one should already smell wrong (what would an OR of 2.5 imply?). Name each error, then compute what the model actually says happens to a two-vehicle crash’s injury probability when the driver is intoxicated, working through the odds.

Tip

Solution

two_vehicle <- tibble(intox_any = c(0, 1), num_vehicles = c(2, 2))

two_vehicle |>
  mutate(
    p_injury = predict(injury_model, newdata = two_vehicle, type = "response"),
    odds     = p_injury / (1 - p_injury)
  )
# A tibble: 2 × 4
  intox_any num_vehicles p_injury  odds
      <dbl>        <dbl>    <dbl> <dbl>
1         0            2    0.478 0.914
2         1            2    0.636 1.75

Error 1: “91% more likely” reads the odds ratio as a probability ratio. An OR of 1.91 raises the odds by 91%: nothing else. Error 2: multiplying the baseline probability by the OR (0.48 × 1.91) is arithmetic on the wrong scale; with an OR of 2.5 it would “predict” 120% of crashes causing injury, which is the tell that probabilities don’t multiply this way.

What actually happens: the sober two-vehicle probability of .48 converts to odds of ≈ 0.91; raise those by 91% to get odds ≈ 1.75; convert back and the probability is ≈ .64. The real change is 48% → 64%: about 16 percentage points, a ~33% relative rise, nowhere near 91%. When the baseline probability is small, odds and probability move almost together; at a baseline near 50%, they part company badly, so change the odds, never the probabilities, and use predict() when you want the probability itself.
