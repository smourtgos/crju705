Logistic regression: odds, odds ratios, and predicted probabilities

Ungraded self-checks. Work each problem in your own R session before clicking the solution. Homework 12 is your own final-project model, so this bank is where you drill the logistic workflow on neutral data first — if you can do these cold, Checkpoint 4 (and the presentation questions afterward) will feel routine.

Problems that use data need only this setup — the 20,000 Alaska crashes from Labs 5, 7, 8, and 12. In lab you modeled whether the driver was intoxicated; here the question flips: does an intoxicated driver make a crash more likely to injure someone? The outcome is a flag we build ourselves:

library(tidyverse)
library(brms)   # only if you re-fit Problem 6's model yourself

crashes <- read_csv("https://smourtgos.github.io/crju705/data/crash-ak.csv") |>
  mutate(injury = as.integer(injuries > 0))   # 1 = someone was injured

Problem 1 · Odds ↔︎ probability, three conversions

No data, just the ladder. (a) About 12% of released defendants in a jurisdiction fail to appear: convert that probability to odds, and phrase it as “1 to ___.” (b) A program director says the odds a participant completes supervision are 4: what probability is that? (c) A logistic model reports a baseline log-odds of −1.99: use plogis() to recover the probability — and notice where you’ve landed.

Tip

Solution

0.12 / (1 - 0.12)   # (a) probability -> odds
[1] 0.1363636
4 / (4 + 1)         # (b) odds -> probability
[1] 0.8
plogis(-1.99)       # (c) log-odds -> probability
[1] 0.1202569
qlogis(0.12)        # and back up the ladder: probability -> log-odds
[1] -1.99243
  1. Odds ≈ 0.136 — about 1 to 7.3: for every failure to appear, roughly seven appearances. (b) Odds of 4 (“four to one”) is a probability of 0.80. (c) plogis(-1.99) ≈ 0.12 — you’ve climbed back down to (a), because −1.99 is exactly qlogis(0.12). Probability, odds, and log-odds are three readouts of one quantity; plogis() and qlogis() move you up and down the ladder, and the model does its arithmetic on the log-odds rung.

Problem 3 · Exponentiate: log-odds → odds ratios

Turn the model into odds ratios with confidence intervals (exp(cbind(OR = coef(...), confint(...)))). Interpret each predictor in one sentence with the holding-constant clause — and state what the “no effect” value is for an odds ratio.

Tip

Solution

exp(cbind(OR = coef(injury_model), confint(injury_model)))
                   OR     2.5 %    97.5 %
(Intercept)  0.568485 0.4994012 0.6468929
intox_any    1.912470 1.7188884 2.1296050
num_vehicles 1.267901 1.1823033 1.3598917
  • Intoxication: OR ≈ 1.91 [1.72, 2.13] — a crash involving an intoxicated driver has about 91% higher odds of injuring someone than a sober-driver crash, holding the number of vehicles constant.
  • Vehicles: OR ≈ 1.27 [1.18, 1.36] — each additional vehicle multiplies the odds of injury by about 1.27. (In these data num_vehicles is only ever 1 or 2, so this is really the two-vehicle vs. single-vehicle contrast — check your predictor’s actual values before narrating a “per unit” story.)
  • For odds ratios the no-effect value is 1, not 0 — both intervals sit clearly above it.

exp() converts every log-odds coefficient into a multiplier on the odds, and 1 is the number that means “nothing happening.”

Problem 4 · From effects to cases: predicted probabilities

Odds ratios describe effects; a briefing needs cases. Use predict(type = "response") for two crash profiles — (i) sober driver, single vehicle; (ii) intoxicated driver, two vehicles — then reproduce profile (i) by hand with plogis() and the coefficients. How do the two probabilities compare with the 48% baseline?

Tip

Solution

profiles <- tibble(intox_any = c(0, 1), num_vehicles = c(1, 2))

profiles |>
  mutate(p_injury = predict(injury_model, newdata = profiles, type = "response"))
# A tibble: 2 × 3
  intox_any num_vehicles p_injury
      <dbl>        <dbl>    <dbl>
1         0            1    0.419
2         1            2    0.636
b <- coef(injury_model)
unname(plogis(b[1] + b[2] * 0 + b[3] * 1))   # profile (i), by hand
[1] 0.4188691

A sober single-vehicle crash carries about a 42% predicted probability of injury; an intoxicated two-vehicle crash about 64% — one below the 48% baseline, one well above it. And the by-hand version demystifies the machinery: plug the profile into the log-odds equation, then plogis() climbs down the ladder to a probability. Report odds ratios for effects and predicted probabilities for cases — decision-makers think in probabilities, and predict() is how you hand them one.

Problem 5 · Audit the memo (the classic mistake)

A draft memo about your model reads:

“The odds ratio for intoxication is 1.91, so a crash with an intoxicated driver is 91% more likely to injure someone. Since about 48% of crashes cause injury, that works out to roughly 0.48 × 1.91 ≈ 92% of intoxicated-driver crashes causing injury.”

Both bolded claims are wrong — and the second one should already smell wrong (what would an OR of 2.5 imply?). Name each error, then compute what the model actually says happens to a two-vehicle crash’s injury probability when the driver is intoxicated, working through the odds.

Tip

Solution

p_sober <- unname(plogis(b[1] + b[3] * 2))          # sober, two vehicles
p_intox <- unname(plogis(b[1] + b[2] + b[3] * 2))   # intoxicated, two vehicles
c(p_sober = p_sober, p_intox = p_intox)
  p_sober   p_intox 
0.4775014 0.6360684 
odds_sober <- p_sober / (1 - p_sober)
odds_intox <- p_intox / (1 - p_intox)
c(odds_sober = odds_sober, odds_intox = odds_intox, ratio = odds_intox / odds_sober)
odds_sober odds_intox      ratio 
 0.9138807  1.7477694  1.9124700

Error 1: “91% more likely” reads the odds ratio as a probability ratio. An OR of 1.91 multiplies the odds by 1.91 — nothing else. Error 2: multiplying the baseline probability by the OR (0.48 × 1.91) is arithmetic on the wrong scale; with an OR of 2.5 it would “predict” 120% of crashes causing injury, which is the tell that probabilities don’t multiply this way.

What actually happens: the sober two-vehicle probability of .48 converts to odds of ≈ 0.91; multiply those by 1.91 to get odds ≈ 1.75; convert back and the probability is ≈ .64. The real change is 48% → 64% — about 16 percentage points, a ~33% relative rise, nowhere near 91%. When the baseline probability is small, odds and probability move almost together; at a baseline near 50%, they part company badly — so multiply odds, never probabilities, and use predict() when you want the probability itself.

Problem 6 · Reading a brms bernoulli output

A classmate fits the Bayesian twin of your Problem 2 model and sends you the summary below. Without fitting anything, answer: (a) what do Family: bernoulli and Links: mu = logit tell you about the scale of the Estimate column? (b) Exponentiate the intox_any estimate and both CI bounds — what odds ratio and 95% credible interval do you get, and does it agree with glm()’s? (c) Are there signs of sampling trouble? (d) Write the one Bayesian sentence about intoxication you’re now licensed to say.

(Want to reproduce it? The code is below — but budget for the “Compiling Stan program…” pause plus a couple of minutes of sampling on 20,000 rows. The reading practice is the point; the refit is optional.)

injury_bayes <- brm(injury ~ intox_any + num_vehicles, data = crashes,
                    family = bernoulli(),
                    chains = 2, iter = 2000, seed = 705, refresh = 0)
summary(injury_bayes)
Tip

Solution

exp(c(estimate = 0.65, ci_low = 0.54, ci_high = 0.76))
estimate   ci_low  ci_high 
1.915541 1.716007 2.138276

  1. bernoulli is brms’s name for a 0/1 outcome and logit is the link — so the Estimate column is in log-odds, exactly like glm()’s coefficient table, and needs exponentiating before anyone speaks.

  2. exp(0.65) ≈ 1.92, with a 95% credible interval of roughly [1.72, 2.14] — landing almost exactly on glm()’s OR of 1.91 [1.72, 2.13], as expected with default priors and 20,000 crashes.

  3. No trouble: Rhat = 1.00 on every row and effective sample sizes in the 1,400–2,100 range mean the two chains agreed and mixed well.

  4. “Given the data and priors, there is a 95% probability that intoxication multiplies a crash’s odds of injuring someone by between roughly 1.7 and 2.1, holding the number of vehicles constant.” Same machinery as every brm() you’ve run — bernoulli is just family = binomial in Bayesian clothing, and the coefficients only become English after exp().
