Practice Problems · Session 6

Hypothesis testing, both ways: p-values, posteriors, and credible intervals

Ungraded self-checks. Work each problem in your own R session before clicking the solution. These use different questions and numbers than the homework on purpose — if you can run and read both traditions cold here, Homework 6 will feel routine.

All problems that use data need only:

library(tidyverse)
source("https://smourtgos.github.io/crju705/R/bayes.R")   # gives you bayes_prop_test()
crimes <- read_csv("https://smourtgos.github.io/crju705/data/chicago-crimes-2025.csv")

Problem 1 · What a p-value promises

A study compares case-processing times in two courts and reports p = 0.03. Three readings:

  1. “There is a 3% chance the courts truly process cases at the same speed.”

  2. “If the courts truly processed cases at the same speed, a gap this large or larger would show up in about 3% of studies like this one.”

  3. “There is a 97% chance the courts differ.”

Pick the correct one. Then verify the machinery’s actual promise by simulation: run 5,000 t-tests comparing two groups drawn from the same normal distribution (n = 40 each) and compute how often p < .05.

Tip

Solution

set.seed(705)
null_world_p <- replicate(5000, t.test(rnorm(40), rnorm(40))$p.value)
mean(null_world_p < 0.05)
[1] 0.0552

(b) — and only (b). A p-value is \(P(\text{data this extreme} \mid H_0)\), a statement about data under an assumed null; (a) and (c) both flip it into \(P(H \mid \text{data})\), which frequentism never computes (that flip is Bayes’ job, and it costs a prior). The simulation shows the promise being kept: in a world where the null is true, about 5.5% of our 5,000 tests came up “significant” — right around the advertised 5% false-alarm rate, and no better.

Problem 2 · Which finding is bigger?

Two studies of body-worn cameras and citizen complaints. Study A (50,000 police stops): 20.8% of stops without a camera drew a complaint vs 20.0% with one, p = 0.03. Study B (40 stops): 70% vs 40%, p = 0.11. A headline writer says A “found an effect” and B “found nothing.” Check both with prop.test() (A: 5,200 of 25,000 vs 5,000 of 25,000; B: 14 of 20 vs 8 of 20), then say what the headline gets wrong.

Tip

Solution

prop.test(x = c(5200, 5000), n = c(25000, 25000))

    2-sample test for equality of proportions with continuity correction

X-squared = 4.8775, df = 1, p-value = 0.02721
alternative hypothesis: two.sided
95 percent confidence interval:
 0.0008961201 0.0151038799
sample estimates:
prop 1 prop 2 
 0.208  0.200 
prop.test(x = c(14, 8), n = c(20, 20))

    2-sample test for equality of proportions with continuity correction

data:  c(14, 8) out of c(20, 20)
X-squared = 2.5253, df = 1, p-value = 0.112
alternative hypothesis: two.sided
95 percent confidence interval:
 -0.0439946  0.6439946
sample estimates:
prop 1 prop 2 
   0.7    0.4

Study A’s gap is 0.8 of a percentage point (interval about 0.1 to 1.5 points): real, probably, and tiny. Study B’s gap is 30 points, with an interval from about 4 points lower to 64 points higher: possibly huge, possibly nothing, because 40 stops cannot tell. The p-value measures how detectable a gap is, and detectability is mostly sample size. It never measures how big the gap is. “Significant” is not “large,” and “not significant” is not “nothing.”

Problem 4 · How big, in real units

Both tests just endorsed the street-vs-residence difference. Before anyone acts on it, say how big it is in words a city council member would follow, with no p-value and no interval. Then say what the tests cannot tell the council.

Tip

Solution

About 17 of every 100 street incidents end in an arrest, against about 10 of every 100 incidents inside a residence: roughly 8 more arrests per 100 incidents. That is the size. Whether 8 per 100 is big enough to matter, and why the gap exists (different crime types happen on the street, officers are already nearby, witnesses are present), no test can say. Both tests tell you the gap is almost surely real. Neither tells you it is important, and neither tells you what causes it.

Problem 5 · The sentence you’re licensed to say

Session 5 forbade “there’s a 95% probability the truth is in this interval.” Using Problem 3’s two outputs, write (1) the honest sentence for the confidence interval and (2) the sentence the credible interval lets you say. The numbers are the same. What is different?

Tip

Solution

  1. Confidence interval: “We estimate the street arrest rate is 7.9 points higher (95% CI: 6.6 to 9.2); the method that built this interval captures the truth 95% of the time.” The 95% belongs to the procedure.

  2. Credible interval: “Given these data, there is a 95% probability that the true difference is between 6.6 and 9.2 points.” The 95% belongs to this interval. That is the sentence everyone wants to say about a confidence interval, and only the Bayesian output licenses it, at the price of a stated prior (here, “know nothing”).

Problem 6 · The diversion-program trial

A county pilots a pretrial diversion program to reduce failures to appear (FTA). Historically, about 25% of defendants fail to appear, and the county trusts that figure about as much as having watched 12 defendants: 3 failed to appear, 9 showed up. That is prior = c(3, 9). The pilot randomizes 120 defendants: of 60 diversion clients, 12 fail to appear; of 60 controls, 19 do.

  1. Run the frequentist test, diversion first: prop.test(x = c(12, 19), n = c(60, 60)).
  2. Run bayes_prop_test() with the same numbers and the prior.
  3. Write one sentence for the county board.
Tip

Solution

prop.test(x = c(12, 19), n = c(60, 60))

    2-sample test for equality of proportions with continuity correction

data:  c(12, 19) out of c(60, 60)
X-squared = 1.5658, df = 1, p-value = 0.2108
alternative hypothesis: two.sided
95 percent confidence interval:
 -0.28856877  0.05523544
sample estimates:
   prop 1    prop 2 
0.2000000 0.3166667 
bayes_prop_test(x = c(12, 19), n = c(60, 60), prior = c(3, 9))

    Bayesian test of two proportions 

data, group 1:  12 of 60 (20.0%)
data, group 2:  19 of 60 (31.7%)
prior:          3 yes, 9 no (about 25%, worth 12 cases), same for both groups

                         estimate      95% credible interval
group 1                  20.8%         12.3% to 30.9%
group 2                  30.6%         20.5% to 41.6%
difference (1 minus 2)   -9.7 points   -23.7 to 4.4 points

Probability group 1 is higher than group 2: 0.09 
Probability group 1 is lower than group 2:  0.91

The frequentist verdict: p ≈ 0.21 — an 11.7-point gap like this could plausibly arise if the program did nothing, so “not significant,” and the grant report probably ends there. The Bayesian output tells the board what it actually asked: given prior experience and the pilot data, there is about a 91% probability the diversion program lowers failure-to-appear (credible interval for the difference about −24 to +4 points). One board-ready sentence: “The pilot is too small for a definitive verdict, but the evidence currently runs about 9-to-1 that diversion reduces failures to appear.” Both statements are true of the same data — they answer different questions, and only one of them helps a decision-maker.
