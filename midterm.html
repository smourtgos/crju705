Midterm Study Guide

Session 9 (Tuesday, October 13) · in class · covers Sessions 1 to 8

The midterm is in class and monitored. You write and troubleshoot real R code. You may use your printed notes and homework: no internet, no AI tools. (This is the assessment the AI policy warns you about: if AI has been writing your homework, this is where it shows.)

How it works mechanically: you get an .R script with the questions plus the files it needs, all distributed together. You answer in the script and submit it. Three things to know in advance:

  • Light data wrangling IS testable. The exam is code-based, so expect to read_csv() a file and use the everyday verbs: filter(), count(), summarize(). Getting data into a testable shape is part of doing statistics.
  • bayes_prop_test() comes in a file. There is no internet during the exam, so the course’s bayes.R file is in the exam folder and you load it with source("bayes.R"). Everything else about it works exactly as in Sessions 6 and 7.
  • Packages: tidyverse and modeest, both installed earlier this semester. Check that library(tidyverse) and library(modeest) both load on your laptop before exam day.

Best preparation: the practice banks for Sessions 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, and 8 (ungraded problems with hidden solutions, in the exam’s style), plus the midterm review at the end of the Session 8 slides.

The six parts

6 Correlation 15

1. Data and measurement

  • Explain the four levels of measurement (nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio)
  • Give an example of each, especially from criminal justice research

2. Descriptive statistics

  • Calculate the mean, median, and mode in R
  • Compute the variance and standard deviation in R
  • Explain what the standard deviation means in plain language
  • Make and interpret a histogram
  • Explain why an outlier moves the mean more than the median or mode

3. Probability and sampling

  • Simulate random events (for example, sample()) and count outcomes with count() or table()
  • The difference between small and large samples (the law of large numbers)
  • Build and interpret a contingency table
  • Calculate a conditional probability from a table, and tell it apart from a joint probability
  • Reverse a conditional with a counting table: base rate + how often the test flags true cases + how often it flags false ones → P(condition | flagged)

4. Confidence intervals and the t-test

  • Compute group means
  • Make and interpret a boxplot
  • Run a two-sample t-test with t.test(outcome ~ group, data = )
  • Read the interval: which group minus which, in what units, and where zero is
  • Tell apart “is the difference detectable?” and “is the difference large?”

5. Frequentist and Bayesian thinking

  • What a p-value means, and what it does not mean (it is never the probability the null is true)
  • How a Bayesian probability statement differs: the probability of a claim given the data
  • Why priors matter, and how ignoring a base rate can mislead
  • Run prop.test() and bayes_prop_test() on the same four numbers, and say what each output lets you conclude

6. Correlation

  • Make and read a scatterplot: direction, strength, a straight line or not, wild points
  • Compute r with cor() and describe it in words
  • Run cor.test(x, y) and read r, the confidence interval, and the p-value. Where is zero?
  • Explain what a correlation cannot tell you: cause, and (when the rows are places) anything about individuals (the ecological fallacy)

General advice

  • Practice running the R code from your homework, labs, and in-class exercises. The midterm only asks you to do things we’ve already done.
  • Make sure you can both run the code and explain in words what the output means.
  • Focus on the big ideas: variability, sampling, inference, comparing groups, and association.
  • Don’t overthink the Bayesian part. It is at the same level as Sessions 6 and 7.

R you should know cold

mean(x); median(x)
library(modeest); mfv(x)                  # mode
var(x); sd(x)

# plots (ggplot as practiced in lab; base equivalents also fine)
ggplot(df, aes(x)) + geom_histogram()
ggplot(df, aes(group, y)) + geom_boxplot()
ggplot(df, aes(x, y)) + geom_point()

# counting and contingency tables
df |> count(x, y)
table(df$x, df$y)

# two groups, a number: t-test and its confidence interval
t.test(y ~ group, data = df)

# two groups, yes/no: both traditions
prop.test(x = c(40, 30), n = c(100, 100))
source("bayes.R")                          # on the exam; in class it came from the course site
bayes_prop_test(x = c(40, 30), n = c(100, 100))

# two numbers: correlation
cor(df$x, df$y)
cor.test(df$x, df$y)

Concepts you should be able to explain

  • Levels of measurement: nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio (with examples)
  • Mean, median, mode: what each tells you; the effect of outliers
  • Variance and SD: measures of spread; the SD is roughly the typical distance from the mean
  • Law of large numbers: small samples bounce around; large samples settle down
  • Contingency tables: totals, joint probability, conditional probability
  • Confidence intervals: zero inside the interval → no detectable difference; zero outside → detectable
  • p-value: the probability of data at least this extreme if the null is true
  • Bayesian probability: the probability of the claim given the data; priors and base rates matter
  • Correlation: r runs from -1 to +1; sign = direction, size = strength; only straight lines; not cause; areas are not people
