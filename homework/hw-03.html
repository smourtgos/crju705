Homework 3 · Probability and Conditional Thinking

CRJU 705 · due before noon Tuesday ahead of Session 4

Instructions: complete the following exercises in a single .R script file. Include comments explaining your code and answers to any interpretive questions. Submit your .R file via Canvas for credit.

Parts 1–3 use the built-in mtcars dataset (type mtcars in the console — it’s always available, no loading needed).

Part 1: Contingency Tables

  1. Create a contingency table of mtcars showing the number of cars by number of cylinders (cyl) and number of gears (gear). Use either the classic table(mtcars$cyl, mtcars$gear) or the tidyverse route from Lab 3:

    library(tidyverse)
mtcars |>
  count(cyl, gear) |>
  pivot_wider(names_from = gear, values_from = n)

  2. How many cars have 4 cylinders and 4 gears?

  3. What is the probability that a randomly selected car (out of all 32) has both 4 gears and 6 cylinders? (This is a joint probability — divide the cell count by the grand total, not by a row or column total.)

  4. What is the probability that a randomly selected car has 6 cylinders? (A marginal probability.)

Part 2: Conditional Probability

  1. What is the conditional probability that a car has 6 cylinders given that it has 4 gears? Compute it in R (not by eye) — e.g., filter() to the condition and take a proportion, or divide the right counts.

  2. Interpretation (in a comment): your answers to Q3 and Q5 use the same cell of the table but give different numbers. Explain in one or two sentences why — what changed between the two questions?

Part 3: Visualizing Distributions

  1. Create a bar plot showing the distribution of cylinders.

  2. Create a bar plot showing the distribution of gears.

  3. Create a grouped bar plot of the number of cars by cyl and gear. You may use ggplot (geom_bar(position = "dodge") — remember to make the grouping variable a factor) or base R’s barplot(..., beside = TRUE) — your choice.

Part 4: Real Data

  1. Load the course anchor data:

    library(tidyverse)
crimes <- read_csv("https://smourtgos.github.io/crju705/data/chicago-crimes-2025.csv")

  2. Compute the marginal probability that an incident ended in arrest: P(arrest). (Hint: arrest is logical, and mean() of a logical is a proportion.)

  3. Compute the conditional probability of arrest given each crime category: P(arrest | Violent), P(arrest | Property), P(arrest | Other). (Hint: summarize() with .by = crime_category.)

  4. Compute P(arrest | domestic == TRUE) and compare it to your overall P(arrest) from Q11.

  5. Interpretation (in a comment): based on Q12 and Q13 — which variable (crime_category or domestic) is closer to independent of arrest in these data, and how can you tell? Use the definition from lecture: A and B are independent when P(A | B) = P(A).

Part 5: Reversing a Conditional

A roadside field drug test has these properties:

  • 5% of stopped drivers actually carry drugs (the base rate)
  • The test flags 90% of drivers who carry (sensitivity)
  • The test also flags 8% of drivers who do not carry (false-positive rate)

  1. For 1,000 stops, build the counting table from lecture — how many carriers get flagged, how many non-carriers get flagged? You can do the arithmetic directly in R (e.g., 1000 * 0.05 * 0.90) or lay it out with tribble(). Report the total number of flagged drivers.

  2. Using your counts from Q15, compute P(carries | flagged) — the probability a flagged driver actually carries.

  3. Interpretation (in a comment): the manufacturer advertises the test as “90% accurate.” In one or two sentences: why is your Q16 answer nowhere near 90%, and which conditional probability does the “90%” actually describe?
