Homework 6 · Bayesian and Frequentist Inference

CRJU 705 · due before noon Tuesday ahead of Session 7

Instructions: submit a single .R script via Canvas. Parts 1 and 2 are written questions — answer each in 3–5 sentences, as comments (#) in your script. Part 3 requires code; include comments interpreting your output. Write in your own words — these questions are about whether you can explain the ideas, not whether you can locate a definition.

Part 1: Core Concepts

  1. In your own words, what does a p-value mean in the frequentist framework?

  2. How is a Bayesian probability statement — e.g., \(P(\text{hypothesis} \mid \text{data})\) — different from a frequentist one?

  3. What role do priors play in Bayesian analysis, and why are they important?

  4. Why might ignoring priors lead to misleading conclusions in criminal justice research? (The Ianfluenza screening example from Session 3 is fair game, but a CJ example of your own is better.)

Part 2: Interpretation & Reflection

  1. In the recidivism example from class, the frequentist test reported p = 0.20, while the Bayesian analysis found a 91% probability that treatment recidivism is lower than control. What do these two results each tell us, and why do they sound so different?

  2. Which result do you think would be easier for policymakers to interpret, and why?

  3. What is one advantage of the frequentist approach over the Bayesian?

  4. What is one advantage of the Bayesian approach over the frequentist?

  5. Think of a criminal justice research example (outside of recidivism) where Bayesian reasoning could give clearer insights than a p-value. Briefly explain.

  6. Imagine you are explaining Bayesian inference to a colleague who has only ever used p-values. How would you summarize the “teaching payoff” in one or two sentences?

Part 3: Both Tests on Real Data

In class we compared violent and property crimes by hour of day. Your question is different: do domestic incidents happen at a different time of day than non-domestic incidents?

  1. Load the packages and the Chicago incident data:

    library(tidyverse)
library(BayesFactor)   # install.packages("BayesFactor") first, if needed

crimes <- read_csv("https://smourtgos.github.io/crju705/data/chicago-crimes-2025.csv")

  2. Compute the mean hour separately for domestic and non-domestic incidents (.by = domestic). In a comment: which group happens earlier in the day, on average, and by roughly how many minutes?

  3. Run the frequentist test:

    t.test(hour ~ domestic, data = crimes)

    In a comment: report the p-value and the 95% confidence interval, then interpret the result in plain English — one sentence a police commander would understand. (Watch the direction: with a logical grouping variable, R computes FALSE minus TRUE.)

  4. Run the Bayesian test on the same question:

    dom_hours    <- crimes$hour[crimes$domestic]
nondom_hours <- crimes$hour[!crimes$domestic]

bf <- ttestBF(x = dom_hours, y = nondom_hours)
extractBF(bf)$bf

    In a comment: report the Bayes factor and interpret it in plain English. Which hypothesis do the data support, and how strongly?

  5. Interpretation (in comments):

    • Do the frequentist and Bayesian results agree here? In class, on the violent-vs-property question, they pointed in different directions — why do you think these two analyses line up (or don’t)?
    • Is this difference practically important? Consider the size of the gap in minutes against the spread of incident times across a 24-hour day, and answer in one or two sentences.
