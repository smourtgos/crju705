Homework 12 · Your Final Model + Project Checkpoint 4

CRJU 705 · due before noon Tuesday ahead of Session 13

Instructions: complete the following in a single .R script file and submit it via Canvas for credit. This week the entire homework is about your final project — there is no practice dataset. Every part below uses the dataset you selected in Session 7 and have been building through Checkpoints 1–3.

(Session 13 is the project help session — no new material, just you, your code, and me. This homework is what makes that hour useful: if Parts 1–3 surface problems, that’s exactly what the help session is for.)

Part 1: Your Cleaned Variables

Run summary() and str() on the cleaned and transformed variables you plan to use in your final model — the outcome and every predictor. Paste the output into your script as comments directly below each call, so I can see exactly what you saw.

# For example, if your model variables live in a cleaned data frame:
model_vars <- my_data |>
  select(outcome_variable, predictor_1, predictor_2, predictor_3)

summary(model_vars)
# (paste the output here as comments)
str(model_vars)
# (paste the output here as comments)

What I’m checking: every variable is the type your model needs (numeric predictors numeric, a binary outcome coded 0/1, logical, or a two-level factor), the values are plausible (no impossible numbers, no accidental character columns), and any missing values are ones you know about.

Part 2: Your Model Equation

Write out, in a comment, the equation you plan to use to answer your research question. For example, if you were running a multiple linear regression model on violent crime, it might look like this:

# ViolentCrime = Intercept + Poverty + HighSchool + SingleParent + Error

Be sure to specify whether you’re running a linear or logistic regression model — and add one sentence on why: what type of variable is your outcome?

It can be that simple. I just want to see that you understand your dependent variable and all of the independent variables you plan to include in the model. Make sure your equation matches the cleaned variables in your Part 1 output — every term in the equation should appear in your summary()/str() results, and nothing extra.

Part 3: Project Checkpoint 4 (final)

This is the last of the four project checkpoints. Checkpoint 1 described your variables, Checkpoint 2 measured their associations, Checkpoint 3 fit a first model — Checkpoint 4 is the real thing: the model you intend to defend at presentations. It is graded on completion and quality of reasoning, not on whether your results are exciting. Null findings, honestly interpreted, are full-credit findings. The help session (Session 13) comes after this checkpoint on purpose: finish it, note what’s shaky, and bring the shaky parts to class.

In a clearly marked section at the end of your .R script (e.g., # ---- PROJECT CHECKPOINT 4 ----):

  1. Fit your final model — in both traditions. The frequentist fit (lm() for a continuous outcome, glm(family = binomial) for a binary one) and the Bayesian fit (brm() with the same formula — default priors are fine, standard settings: chains = 2, iter = 2000, seed = 705, refresh = 0; expect the compile pause). Both fits must match your Part 2 equation. (This is the final-project requirement — see Step 3 — so Checkpoint 4 is where you get it running with a week of slack.)

  2. Interpret the key coefficients in comments, on the right scale:

    • Linear: the change in your outcome per one-unit change in each predictor, holding the others constant — with units.
    • Logistic: exponentiate. Odds ratios per one-unit change, holding the others constant — and at least one predicted probability for a concrete, meaningful case, as we did in class.
    • Include the uncertainty from both fits — the 95% CI and the 95% credible interval — plus one sentence comparing them: do the two traditions agree here, and what does the posterior let you say that the p-value doesn’t?

  3. Produce ONE table or figure you plan to show at presentations. A tidy coefficient table, a plot of predicted values or probabilities, a well-labeled scatterplot with your fitted line — your choice, but it must be presentation-ready: real variable names, labeled axes or columns, a title. Not raw console output.

  4. Write the three sentences you’d say about it to a non-statistician. As a comment block. Imagine a police chief, a judge, or a city council member: no “log-odds,” no “p < .05,” no Greek letters. What did you find, how sure are you, and why should they care? These three sentences are the spine of your Session 14 presentation — write them now and presentation prep gets much easier.

If anything above breaks — the model won’t run, a coefficient makes no sense, the output contradicts Checkpoint 3 — say so in your comments and bring it to the help session. Finding that out now, with a week to fix it, is precisely what this checkpoint is for.

If You Get Stuck

If you run into problems cleaning or transforming your data, email me the code you’re using (copy and paste into the email is fine — no need to attach a script), along with a short explanation of what you’re trying to do and what the issue is. I’m happy to help with the data-cleaning process. There’s usually a simple fix that’s easy to miss when you’re still learning R.
