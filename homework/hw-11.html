Homework 11 · Cleaning Data for Regression + Project Checkpoint 3

CRJU 705 · due before noon Tuesday ahead of Session 12

Instructions: complete the following exercises in a single .R script file. Your script must run top-to-bottom when I execute it, with answers to interpretive questions in #comments. Submit your .R file via Canvas for credit.

As you begin working with your final-project data, you will quickly discover that most of your time is spent cleaning the data — getting it into a shape where a model will even run, and where the numbers it returns mean what you think they mean. That’s not a detour from analysis; it is analysis. This homework makes the point the honest way: you’ll clean a dirty file, then watch what regression does before and after you cleaned it.

Do not shortcut Part 1. Every minute you spend on these verbs now is an hour saved on your final project. (If any verb feels shaky, the reference chapter is R4DS Ch. 3, Data transformation — the same toolkit from Labs 3–6.)

Setup

library(tidyverse)
library(brms)
library(car)

officers <- read_csv("https://smourtgos.github.io/crju705/data/officers.csv")
glimpse(officers)

The file holds survey records for 200 correctional officers (simulated, but realistically messy): facility, shift, tenure, monthly overtime hours, hours of sleep, and a burnout score that is supposed to run 0–100. Your eventual model: burnout ~ overtime_hours_mo + sleep_hours.

Part 1: Find the Dirt, Fix the Dirt

  1. First pass. Run summary() on the data (or on the numeric columns). In a comment: identify two impossible values hiding in variables your model needs. (Hint: check each variable’s minimum and maximum against what the variable is.)

  2. Categorical hygiene. Run count(officers, shift). In a comment: what went wrong in data entry? Fix it with mutate() + str_to_title(), then re-run count() to confirm exactly three shifts remain — and report the three counts.

  3. Sentinels to NA. The burnout instrument’s codebook says 999 = “declined to answer.” That is a code, not a score — left as a number, it will poison every mean, SD, and slope downstream. Convert it: mutate(burnout = na_if(burnout, 999)). Then deal with the impossible overtime value the same general way (negative hours cannot happen; make it NA — if_else() is one route). In a comment: how many NAs does each of your two repaired variables now contain?

  4. Build the analysis file. Create officers_clean by dropping the rows with NA on any model variable: drop_na(burnout, overtime_hours_mo, sleep_hours). In a comment: how many rows did you lose, and out of how many?

  5. Grouped summary practice. On officers_clean, compute the mean burnout score and the number of officers per facility (summarize() with .by = facility), and arrange() the result in descending order. In a comment: which facility reports the highest average burnout, and is the gap between facilities large relative to burnout’s SD?

Part 2: Regression, Before and After Cleaning

  1. Fit the dirty model. On the original officers data, fit lm(burnout ~ overtime_hours_mo + sleep_hours). Look at summary(), then make the residuals-vs-fitted plot from Lab 11. In a comment: what in the plot (and in the residual standard error) screams that something is wrong? Remember the outcome is supposed to live between 0 and 100.

  2. Fit the clean model. Same formula on officers_clean. In a comment: compare the residual standard error across the two fits, and explain why a handful of 999s did that much damage. (Think about what least squares does to a residual of ~900 when it squares it.)

  3. Interpret the clean model honestly. Look at the coefficients and confint(), and run vif(). In comments:

    • One holding-constant sentence per predictor — including the direction and size of each estimated coefficient.
    • Neither predictor reaches conventional significance in this sample. Write the honest version of that finding in one sentence — what the data do and don’t show about overtime, sleep, and burnout here. (“We found no reliable association…” is a legitimate scientific result, not a failure. You will need this sentence again in your career, probably often.)
    • What does the VIF tell you about these two predictors?

  4. The Bayesian check. Fit the clean model with brms:

    burnout_bayes <- brm(burnout ~ overtime_hours_mo + sleep_hours,
                     data = officers_clean,
                     chains = 2, iter = 2000, seed = 705, refresh = 0)
summary(burnout_bayes)

    In a comment: do the 95% credible intervals include 0? Write one Bayesian sentence about the sleep coefficient, and say whether the two traditions agree here.

Part 3: Project Checkpoint 3

This section uses YOUR final-project dataset. Checkpoint 3 of 4: you described your variables in Checkpoint 1 and explored the bivariate relationships in Checkpoint 2 — now you fit the first regression model of your project. Like the others, it is graded on completion and quality of reasoning — not on whether the model comes out significant, and not on polish. A model that comes out null, weird, or broken — noticed and discussed — is a successful checkpoint.

In a clearly marked section at the end of your .R script (e.g., # ---- PROJECT CHECKPOINT 3 ----):

  1. Fit a first regression model: your outcome regressed on 2–3 predictors (the ones your Checkpoint 2 exploration made most promising).

    • If your outcome is continuous (or close to it), use lm() — this is your tool.
    • If your outcome is binary (yes/no, arrest/no arrest, recidivated/didn’t), say so in a comment — the right tool is logistic regression, which is exactly what Session 12 covers. For now, fit the linear model anyway as a rough first pass (with a binary outcome its coefficients read approximately as changes in the probability of a “yes”), and label it clearly as a placeholder you’ll replace next week.

  2. Report and interpret the coefficients: one plain-language, holding-constant sentence per predictor, with units — plus one sentence on R² (or note that R² will make more sense post-logistic if your outcome is binary).

  3. One diagnostic check: a residuals-vs-fitted plot or vif() (VIF only makes sense with 2+ predictors — running both earns no extra credit but will earn my respect). One comment on what it shows.

  4. Write a short paragraph (comment block, roughly 5–8 sentences): what does this first model say about your research question? Which coefficient is most interesting, and is its direction what you predicted in Checkpoint 1? Name one lurking variable your model cannot hold constant because your dataset doesn’t measure it. And if anything looks broken — a nonsense coefficient, an outcome that clearly isn’t continuous, residuals doing something alarming — say so plainly and come see me. Catching it now is the checkpoint working.
